Quiz: ethics of genetic knowledge

Short questions on the facts from the lecture, and on applying the four principles. For the “apply” questions there is often more than one defensible answer: the answer below shows the reasoning we look for, not the only conclusion.

Q1. Name the four principles of biomedical ethics, and give each in a few words.

Hint

One is about the person’s own choice, two are about good and harm, one is about fairness.

Answer
  • Autonomy: respect a person’s own informed, voluntary choices.
  • Beneficence: act for the person’s good.
  • Non-maleficence: do no harm (or avoid harm that is not outweighed by benefit).
  • Justice: distribute benefits, risks and costs fairly.

They come from Beauchamp and Childress, Principles of Biomedical Ethics. In real cases they conflict, and the work is to weigh them.

Q2. Give three reasons why a genetic test result raises ethical questions that a blood glucose value does not.

Hint

Think of relatives, the future, and what a genome can be used for besides medicine.

Answer

Any three of:

  • It is shared with relatives (½ of alleles with a parent, child or sibling), so it tells something about people who were not asked.
  • It can predict disease decades ahead, sometimes with no treatment (Huntington disease).
  • It is permanent and identifying: a genome cannot be changed, and can identify a person even without a name.
  • It informs reproductive choices about future people (carrier and prenatal tests).
  • Its meaning changes as variants are reclassified.

Q3. Anna’s mother has Huntington disease. Anna is 30, healthy, and does not want to be tested. Her daughter Elsa, 20, wants to be tested. (a) What is Elsa’s prior risk of carrying the HTT expansion? (b) If Elsa tests positive, what does it reveal about Anna? (c) Which principles are in conflict?

Hint

Huntington disease is autosomal dominant. Elsa can only have inherited the allele through Anna.

Answer

  1. Anna has a ½ chance of carrying the allele, and if she does, Elsa has a ½ chance: ½ × ½ = ¼.

  2. A positive result in Elsa means Anna must carry the allele too: she is the only route from the grandmother. Anna learns her own status against her will.

  3. Elsa’s autonomy (her right to know about her own future) against Anna’s autonomy in the form of the right not to know, and non-maleficence towards Anna. Genetic counselling would try to find a way through, for example by discussing with Elsa how the result will be kept, and whether she can wait or talk with her mother first. There is no rule that one side always wins.

Q4. What is the difference between an incidental finding and a secondary finding? Give a genetic example of each.

Hint

One is found by chance; the other is looked for on purpose, although it is not why the test was ordered.

Answer

An incidental finding is discovered by chance and is unrelated to the question, for example misattributed paternity discovered when a trio (child and both parents) is sequenced to explain a child’s disease.

A secondary finding is deliberately looked for in a list of actionable genes, although the test was ordered for something else. Example: reporting a pathogenic BRCA1 variant found while sequencing an exome to explain epilepsy. The ACMG publishes the list of such genes (ACMG SF v3.2, Miller et al. 2023, PMID 37347242; later updated to v3.3).

Q5. NIPT is described as a screening test, while amniocentesis gives a diagnostic test. What does NIPT analyse, and why does a positive NIPT result need to be confirmed?

Hint

Where does the DNA come from, and is it all from the fetus?

Answer

NIPT analyses cell-free DNA in the pregnant woman’s blood, part of which comes from the fetus (more exactly, mostly from the placenta). It estimates whether, for example, chromosome 21 is over-represented. Because the DNA is a mixture, and the placenta’s DNA does not always match the fetus, the result is a risk, not a diagnosis. A positive result is confirmed by testing fetal cells (amniocentesis or CVS) before any decision is made.

Presence of fetal DNA in maternal plasma was first shown by Lo et al. 1997 (Lancet, PMID 9274585).

Q6. In Gymrek et al. (2013), how were the identities of “anonymous” research participants recovered? Which mode of inheritance from the pedigree unit made it possible?

Hint

Which chromosome travels with the surname in many families?

Answer

The authors read short tandem repeats on the Y chromosome (Y-STRs) from the genomes, and searched public recreational genealogy databases, where men upload their Y-STR profile with their surname. A match gives a likely surname; combined with metadata such as age and US state, this narrowed the search down to one person.

It works because of Y-linked inheritance: the Y passes from father to every son, as the surname does in many families. Gymrek et al., Science 339:321–324, PMID 23329047.

Q7. A man has never taken a DNA test. Police identify him from a crime-scene sample through a genealogy database. How is that possible, and whose consent is missing?

Hint

Think of what you share with a third cousin, and who uploaded their DNA.

Answer

His relatives uploaded their DNA. Distant cousins share long identical stretches of DNA, which a SNP array detects. From the matches, genealogists build family trees down to a few candidates, and a direct DNA test confirms. This is how the Golden State Killer suspect was found in April 2018. Erlich et al. (2018, PMID 30309907) projected that about 60% of searches for people of European descent would give a third-cousin or closer match.

Missing consent: the relatives agreed to use the database for genealogy, not as police informants; the suspect never agreed to anything. In Sweden, a law in force from 1 July 2025 allows the police to use the method only for murder and aggravated rape, as a last resort.

Q8. Why is editing a human embryo ethically different from editing a patient’s blood stem cells, even when the same gene and the same CRISPR method are used?

Hint

Where does the edit end up, and who inherits it?

Answer

Editing an embryo is germline editing: every cell of the person, including the eggs or sperm, carries the edit, so it is inherited by future generations by Mendel’s rules. Those descendants cannot consent, and mistakes (off-target cuts, mosaicism) cannot be withdrawn. Editing a patient’s own blood stem cells is somatic: it affects only that consenting person, like any other therapy (e.g. exa-cel for sickle cell disease). In Sweden, research and treatment that cause heritable genetic changes in humans are prohibited (Genetic Integrity Act 2006:351, ch. 2).

Q9. A gene drive allele is present in a heterozygote. Under Mendel’s law of segregation, what fraction of its gametes should carry the drive? What does a homing gene drive do instead, and why is that ethically different from editing a single organism?

Hint

Recall the cross Aa × aa from the Mendel unit.

Answer

Mendel: ½ of the gametes. A homing drive encodes Cas9 and a guide RNA that cut the wild-type homolog in the germline; repair copies the drive across, so the heterozygote passes it to nearly all gametes. The allele spreads through a population even if it harms its carriers: Kyrou et al. (2018, PMID 30247490) saw a drive in doublesex reach 100% prevalence in caged Anopheles gambiae within 7–11 generations, collapsing the population.

Ethically, a release affects a whole wild species and ecosystem, crosses national borders, and cannot easily be recalled. Who has the right to decide (justice), and how do you weigh huge potential benefits (malaria control) against uncertain harms?

Q10. Under the Swedish Ethical Review Act (2003:460), which of these research projects need ethical approval? (a) A study of anonymous, aggregated allele counts downloaded from gnomAD. (b) Genotyping blood samples from named patients, stored in a hospital biobank. (c) An interview study on how people experience predictive testing for Huntington disease, recording participants’ health information.

Hint

The act is triggered by sensitive personal data, physical interventions or methods affecting people, and biological material that can be traced to a person.

Answer
  • (a) No: aggregate counts are not personal data, and no person or sample is involved.
  • (b) Yes: biological material that can be traced to living persons, and genetic data, which is sensitive personal data. The Biobanks Act also applies to the samples.
  • (c) Yes: it processes sensitive personal data (health). Interviews are not a physical intervention, but the data trigger the act.

Applications go to the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Etikprövningsmyndigheten). Work done only as part of a first- or second-cycle course is not “research” under the act, but a student working inside a research project is covered by that project’s approval.

Q11. Between 1935 and 1975, nearly 63,000 sterilisations were reported in Sweden under the sterilisation laws. Apart from the violation of consent, why was the eugenic aim of reducing recessive diseases also genetically mistaken?

Hint

For a rare recessive allele, where are most copies of the allele: in affected people (aa) or in carriers (Aa)?

Answer

For a rare recessive allele with frequency q, affected people (aa) make up about q² of the population and carriers (Aa) about 2q. For q = 0.01, that is 1 in 10,000 affected but about 1 in 50 carriers: almost all copies of the allele are in healthy carriers, who were not targeted. Preventing affected people from reproducing therefore reduces q only very slowly. (You can compute this with Hardy–Weinberg in Part 7.)

The sterilisation laws (1934:171 and 1941:282) were replaced in 1975 by a law that allows sterilisation only at a person’s own request. 93% of those sterilised in 1935–1975 were women.

Q12. A man has familial hypercholesterolaemia (autosomal dominant, treatable with lipid-lowering drugs from childhood) and refuses to tell his siblings. Compare this with a man with Huntington disease who refuses to tell his siblings. Why do the principles weigh differently in the two cases? Should his 12-year-old child be tested?

Hint

In both cases each sibling has a ½ prior risk. What can a sibling do with the information in each case?

Answer

The genetics is the same: each sibling has a ½ risk. The difference is what knowing allows. For FH, early treatment prevents serious, avoidable harm, so beneficence and non-maleficence towards the siblings weigh heavily against his confidentiality; a family letter through the patient, and repeated counselling, are the usual routes, and disclosure without consent is at most a last resort. For Huntington disease there is no cure, so the siblings’ right not to know counts strongly and there is little harm to prevent: his refusal weighs more. The child: FH is treated from childhood, so testing now serves the child’s own interest (with parental consent); for an adult-onset condition without treatment in childhood, testing would normally wait until the child can decide.
