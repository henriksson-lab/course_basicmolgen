Quiz: genomic variation

Short questions on kinds of variants, haplotypes and LD, genotyping and population differences. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. Name the type of each variant: (a) a C → T change at one position; (b) the loss of three bases CTT in CFTR; (c) 42 instead of 20 copies of CAG in HTT; (d) a 3-Mb segment of chromosome 22 present in one copy instead of two.

Hint

SNP, indel, STR (microsatellite) or CNV?

Answer
  1. SNP (single-nucleotide variant). (b) Indel (a 3-bp deletion; in frame, so one amino acid is lost: F508del). (c) STR / microsatellite repeat expansion (Huntington disease range). (d) CNV, here a deletion (the 22q11.2 deletion, as in DiGeorge syndrome).

Q2. Why do microsatellites (STRs) have many more alleles per locus than SNPs?

Hint

Compare their mutation rates and how they mutate.

Answer

STRs mutate by slippage of the DNA polymerase on the repeat during replication, which adds or removes a repeat unit. Their mutation rate is about 10−3–10−4 per locus per generation, against ~10−8 per base for SNPs. Each mutation can produce a new repeat number, so many alleles accumulate. A SNP site usually has only two alleles, because a second mutation at the same base is very rare.

Q3. dbSNP lists rs334 as T>A on chromosome 11; the clinical literature calls HbS c.20A>T. Are these different variants?

Hint

On which strand of chromosome 11 is HBB?

Answer

No, it is the same change. Genome databases report alleles on the plus strand of the chromosome; HBB is on the minus strand, so the coding-sequence (c.) name uses the complementary base: plus-strand T>A = coding-strand A>T.

Q4. What is a haplotype, and why are SNPs that are close together often in linkage disequilibrium?

Hint

How often does a crossover fall between two sites 1 kb apart, compared with two sites 10 Mb apart?

Answer

A haplotype is the set of alleles along one chromosome. When a new allele arises, it sits on one particular haplotype. Crossovers between sites very close together are rare (1 cM ≈ 1 Mb in humans, so about 10−5 per meiosis for sites 1 kb apart), so even after many generations the new allele is still found with the same neighbouring alleles: they are associated, in LD. For distant sites, crossovers have had time to mix the alleles, and the association disappears.

Q5. A consumer genetic test (SNP array) reports that you are not a carrier of any cystic fibrosis alleles on its panel. Does that mean you cannot be a carrier?

Hint

What can an array detect?

Answer

No. An array tests only the known variants it was designed for, typically the most common ones (such as F508del). Hundreds of other CFTR variants can cause cystic fibrosis, including rare ones found in only a few families. Only sequencing the gene would find those. The result lowers your carrier probability; it does not make it zero.

Q6. In the 1000 Genomes data, the lactase persistence allele (rs4988235 A) has frequency 0.74 in CEU and 0 in YRI. Does that mean that no Africans can digest milk as adults?

Hint

Is this the only allele that gives lactase persistence? Which African populations are in 1000 Genomes?

Answer

No. Several pastoralist populations in East Africa (and the Middle East) have other variants in the same LCT enhancer that also cause lactase persistence: the same trait evolved independently (convergent evolution). The African populations in 1000 Genomes (Yoruba, Esan, Mende, Gambian, Luhya) are mostly not traditional milk-drinking herders. One SNP is not the whole story of a trait.

Q7. Lactase persistence is dominant. In the Finnish sample (FIN) the persistence allele has frequency 0.59. What fraction of Finns would you expect to be lactase persistent, if genotypes follow Hardy–Weinberg proportions?

Hint

Persistent = at least one persistence allele = 1 − P(two non-persistence alleles).

Answer

P(no persistence allele) = (1 − 0.59)2 = 0.412 = 0.168, so P(persistent) = 1 − 0.168 ≈ 0.83. About 17% would be expected to be lactase non-persistent.

Q8. Why is the HbS allele common in parts of Africa, although HbS/HbS causes a severe disease?

Hint

Compare the survival of the three genotypes where malaria is common.

Answer

Heterozygote advantage (balancing selection). Where falciparum malaria is common, HbA/HbS carriers are protected against severe malaria and survive better than HbA/HbA people, while HbS/HbS people have sickle-cell disease. Selection against HbS in homozygotes is balanced by selection for it in heterozygotes, so both alleles stay in the population, at about 10–14% HbS in the West and East African 1000 Genomes samples.

Q9. About what share of human genetic variation lies within populations rather than between them? Why, then, do a few alleles such as rs4988235 differ so strongly between populations?

Hint

Most variants are neutral. What forces can change the frequency of one allele a lot?

Answer

About 85–90% of variation is within populations. Most variants are neutral and their frequencies differ only modestly between populations, through drift and migration. Large differences at single loci, against that background, usually point to natural selection in some populations: positive selection for lactase persistence where milk was drunk, balancing selection for HbS where malaria was common.

Q10. A patient has a severe dominant disease with a prevalence of 1 in 100,000. Sequencing finds a missense variant in a candidate gene. In gnomAD the variant has an allele frequency of 0.5% in the patient’s ancestry group. Is it likely to cause the disease?

Hint

For a dominant disease, how common could a fully penetrant causal allele be at most?

Answer

Very unlikely. If the variant caused a fully penetrant dominant disease, about 2 × 0.005 = 1% of people in that group would be heterozygous and affected, a thousand times more than the prevalence of 1 in 100,000. A variant that common in healthy people is almost certainly benign (or at most has a very low penetrance). This is why population frequency is one of the first filters in clinical variant interpretation.

Q11. Two SNPs are about 500 kb apart, so they recombine with r ≈ 0.005 per generation. A new allele at one of them arose in complete LD with its neighbour. What fraction of the original D is left after 200 generations? And if the two SNPs were on different chromosomes?

Hint

Dt = D0 (1 − r)t. Unlinked loci have r = 0.5.

Answer

0.995200 ≈ 0.37: about a third of the association is left after some 5,000 years. For unlinked loci, D halves every generation: 0.5200 is essentially zero, and already after 10 generations less than 0.1% is left. LD between distant sites is short-lived; LD between close sites can last for thousands of generations.

Q12. In a study of 50 people from one population, an allele was not seen at all. What is its allele number, and what is the most you can say about its frequency in that population?

Hint

Count chromosomes, not people. If an event is seen zero times in n tries, its probability is very likely below about 3/n.

Answer

Allele number = 2 × 50 = 100 chromosomes, allele count 0. The frequency could still be up to about 3/100 = 3% (an approximate 95% upper bound, the “rule of three”). “Not seen” in a small sample means “rare”, not “absent”.
