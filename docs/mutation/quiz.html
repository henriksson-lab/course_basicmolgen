Quiz: Mutation & complementation

Short questions on kinds of mutation, mutagens, the fluctuation test, dominance and the complementation test. Have the genetic code table at hand (Hartwell, or the Exercise in R table).

Q1. Classify each substitution as a transition or a transversion: (a) A→G, (b) C→A, (c) T→C, (d) G→T.

Hint

Purines: A, G. Pyrimidines: C, T. Does the base stay in its class?

Answer
  1. A→G: purine to purine, transition. (b) C→A: pyrimidine to purine, transversion. (c) T→C: pyrimidine to pyrimidine, transition. (d) G→T: purine to pyrimidine, transversion.

Q2. The codon TGG (Trp) mutates at one position. Which single-base changes give a nonsense mutation, and which give a missense mutation?

Hint

The stop codons are TAA, TAG and TGA. Try changing each position of TGG.

Answer

Nonsense: TGG → TAG (2nd position G→A) and TGG → TGA (3rd position G→A). Every other single change is missense, because TGG is the only codon for Trp: for example TGG → TGT (Cys), CGG (Arg), GGG (Gly), TTG (Leu), TCG (Ser), TGC (Cys). No change of TGG is silent.

Q3. Why does a single-base deletion near the start of a coding sequence usually destroy the protein, while a deletion of three bases often does not?

Hint

The ribosome reads in triplets from the start codon, with no punctuation.

Answer

Deleting one base shifts the reading frame: every codon after the deletion is read in a different frame, giving a run of wrong amino acids that usually ends at a premature stop codon. Deleting three bases removes one codon (or changes two codons into one) but keeps the frame, so the rest of the protein is normal. The most common cystic fibrosis allele, F508del, is such an in-frame deletion of three bases; the protein is made but folds badly.

Q4. Luria and Delbrück plated 20 small parallel cultures of E. coli on phage T1. Most plates had 0–2 resistant colonies, but one had 107. Why does this result argue against “the phage induces resistance”?

Hint

If each cell had the same small chance of becoming resistant only on the plate, how much should the counts vary between cultures?

Answer

If phage induced resistance on the plate, every cell in every culture would have the same small chance of becoming resistant, so the counts per culture would follow a Poisson distribution, with variance about equal to the mean: no culture should have 107 when the others have 0–2. A jackpot like 107 is expected if a resistance mutation arose early in the growth of that culture, before it met phage, so that the mutant cell left a large clone. Very skewed counts (variance much larger than the mean) mean that mutations arise at random during growth, and the phage only selects them. (The numbers in this question are invented.)

Q5. In the Ames test, why are the bacteria treated with rat liver extract as well as the chemical? Why does the test count reversions rather than forward mutations?

Hint

What does the liver do to foreign chemicals? On which medium can only revertants grow?

Answer

Many chemicals are not mutagenic themselves, but liver enzymes convert them into mutagens. The extract imitates this metabolism, so the test also catches these chemicals. Reversions of a his− mutation can be selected: on a plate without histidine only revertants form colonies, so one revertant among about 108 cells is easy to see. A forward mutation in a random gene would have no such selection and would be hard to find.

Q6. A mutation in an enzyme gene is recessive. Explain why, in terms of the amount of enzyme. Give one reason a loss-of-function allele can instead be dominant.

Hint

How much enzyme does a heterozygote make, and how much does the cell need?

Answer

A heterozygote makes about half the normal amount of enzyme, and for most enzymes half is enough to keep the pathway going, so the phenotype is wild type: the allele is recessive. A loss-of-function allele is dominant if half the product is not enough (haploinsufficiency), or if the faulty product interferes with the normal one, for example as a defective subunit of a complex (dominant negative).

Q7. Why can only recessive mutations be used in a complementation test?

Hint

What does the F1 look like if one of the two mutations is dominant?

Answer

The test reads the F1 phenotype: wild type means each parent supplied what the other lacked. If one mutation is dominant, the F1 is mutant whatever the other mutation is, because one copy of the dominant allele is enough to give the mutant phenotype. So the F1 is mutant even when the two mutations are in different genes, and the test cannot tell them apart.

Q8. Four recessive mutants with yellow leaves give these F1 (+ = green, − = yellow): 1×2 −, 1×3 +, 1×4 +, 2×3 +, 2×4 +, 3×4 −. How many genes? Which mutants are in each?

Hint

Group together mutants that fail to complement (−).

Answer

1 and 2 fail to complement: one gene. 3 and 4 fail to complement: a second gene. Every cross between the pairs is +. So two complementation groups, {1, 2} and {3, 4}: at least two genes are needed for green leaves.

Q9. In Q8, a fifth mutant fails to complement mutants 1 and 3 (and, as it turns out, 2 and 4). Suggest an explanation.

Hint

A simple point mutation hits one gene. What kind of mutation could knock out two genes at once?

Answer

Mutant 5 cannot be a simple recessive mutation in one gene, because then it would fail to complement only one group. Likely explanations: a deletion that removes both genes (if they lie close together), or a dominant mutation (which gives a mutant F1 with anything). A test cross of mutant 5 with wild type distinguishes these: a dominant mutation gives a mutant F1 even with wild type, a recessive deletion does not. Deletions and other rearrangements are in Part 5.

Q10. Two recessive mutations, m1 and m2, give the same phenotype. A diploid with m1 + / + m2 (trans) is mutant, and a diploid with m1 m2 / + + (cis) is wild type. What do you conclude? What could you conclude if the cis diploid were mutant too?

Hint

How many intact copies of the gene does each diploid have? What does the cis diploid test?

Answer

The trans diploid has no intact copy of the gene if both mutations are in the same gene, so a mutant trans result means same gene (they fail to complement). The cis diploid has one completely intact copy, so a wild-type cis result shows that the mutations are recessive and the test is valid. If the cis diploid were also mutant, one intact copy would not be enough: at least one mutation acts dominantly, and the trans result says nothing about whether they are in the same gene.

Q11. Three Neurospora mutants need arginine. Mutant X grows on citrulline or arginine; mutant Y grows only on arginine; mutant Z grows on ornithine, citrulline or arginine. In which order are their blocks in the pathway precursor → ornithine → citrulline → arginine?

Hint

A mutant grows on every compound made after its block.

Answer

Z is blocked earliest (before ornithine), since ornithine rescues it. X is blocked between ornithine and citrulline (citrulline rescues it, ornithine does not). Y is blocked at the last step, citrulline → arginine. The order of blocks is Z, X, Y. Each blocked step is one enzyme and, if the three are in different complementation groups, one gene: Beadle and Tatum’s one gene–one enzyme.
