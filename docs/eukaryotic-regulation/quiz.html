Quiz: Eukaryotic regulation & epigenetics

Short questions on enhancers, transcription factors, chromatin, imprinting and X inactivation. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. A point mutation in an enhancer of gene G is heterozygous. Expression of G drops in the liver but not in the kidney. Does the mutation act in cis or in trans? How could you show it with an allele-specific measurement?

Hint

Think of lacOc. Which copy of G does the enhancer control?

Answer

In cis. An enhancer is a DNA site; it controls only the copy of G on the same chromosome. If the two alleles of G differ at a neutral SNP in the transcript, you can measure each allele’s mRNA in liver (allele-specific expression): the allele on the mutant chromosome will be low and the other normal. A trans-acting mutation (in a transcription factor) would lower both alleles equally. Kidney is unaffected because a different enhancer drives G there.

Q2. GATA1 binds the motif (A/T)GATA(A/G). How often would you expect this 6-bp motif by chance on one strand of random DNA with equal base frequencies? What does that tell you about predicting binding sites from sequence alone?

Hint

Two positions allow 2 of 4 bases; four positions allow 1 of 4.

Answer

P(match at one position) = ½ × (¼)4 × ½ = 1/1024, so about once per kb on each strand: millions of matches in a 3-Gb genome. Only a small fraction are bound in any cell. Binding depends on chromatin accessibility, neighbouring factors and spacing, not on the motif alone.

Q3. Why is lactase persistence (an enhancer variant) inherited as a dominant trait?

Hint

How many active LCT alleles does a heterozygote have in adult gut?

Answer

The variant keeps the enhancer active in adults, so the LCT allele on the same chromosome is still expressed after weaning. A heterozygote has one active allele, which makes enough lactase to digest milk. One copy is enough, so the trait is dominant (a gain of expression in cis).

Q4. Which marks go with active chromatin and which with silent chromatin: H3K27me3, histone acetylation, H3K4me3, methylated CpG islands at the promoter, H3K9me3?

Hint

Two of them are “on”, three are “off”.

Answer

Active: histone acetylation and H3K4me3 (at active promoters). Silent: H3K9me3, H3K27me3 and methylated CpG islands.

Q5. Explain how a DNA methylation pattern can be copied when a cell divides.

Hint

CpG reads the same on both strands. What does each daughter double helix look like right after replication?

Answer

CpG is palindromic, so a methylated site has a methyl-C on both strands. After semiconservative replication each daughter helix is hemi-methylated: the old strand is methylated, the new strand not. The maintenance methyltransferase DNMT1 recognises hemi-methylated CpG and methylates the new strand, restoring the full pattern in both daughters.

Q6. A child has a deletion of 15q11–q13 on the maternal chromosome 15. Which syndrome do you expect, and why? What if the same deletion were on the paternal chromosome?

Hint

Which genes in the region are expressed only from the mother’s copy?

Answer

Angelman syndrome. UBE3A is expressed only from the maternal allele in neurons (the paternal copy is silenced). Losing the maternal copy leaves no active UBE3A in the brain. The same deletion on the paternal 15 removes the paternally expressed genes (the SNRPN cluster) and gives Prader–Willi syndrome.

Q7. How many Barr bodies are in the cells of: a 46,XX woman; a 46,XY man; a 47,XXY man; a 45,X woman; a 47,XXX woman?

Hint

All X chromosomes but one are inactivated.

Answer

X − 1 in each case: 46,XX 1; 46,XY 0; 47,XXY 1; 45,X 0; 47,XXX 2.

Q8. Why are calico (orange-and-black) cats almost always female? What karyotype would you predict for a rare male calico?

Hint

Where is the orange gene?

Answer

The orange locus is on the X. An orange-and-black coat needs both alleles, XO and Xo, active in different patches, so the cat needs two X chromosomes and X inactivation. A normal male (XY) has one X and is either orange or black. A male calico is most likely XXY (or a chimera of two embryos).

Q9. A woman is heterozygous for a recessive X-linked mutation that causes haemophilia A in males. She has a clear bleeding tendency. Give two possible explanations.

Hint

Which X is active in the cells that make clotting factor VIII?

Answer

Skewed X inactivation: by chance (few progenitor cells at the time of inactivation) or by selection, most of her factor-VIII-producing cells use the mutant X. She is a manifesting carrier. Other possibilities: she has only one X (45,X, Turner), or she carries a second mutation on her other X. (In a pedigree, this looks like reduced penetrance of an X-linked recessive allele in females.)

Q10. In the demo, why does skewing become less common as the number of progenitor cells N grows?

Hint

Each progenitor is a coin toss. What happens to the proportion of heads as you toss more coins?

Answer

The fraction of progenitors that chose the maternal X is a binomial proportion with standard deviation \(\sqrt{0.25/N}\). With N = 4 it is 0.25, so 80 : 20 or worse is common (12.5 % of females have all four progenitors choosing the same X); with N = 64 it is 0.0625, and 80 : 20 is many standard deviations away. It is the same law of large numbers as Mendel’s large counts in Part 1.

Q11. Mutations in the Drosophila gene msl-2 kill males but not females. Explain.

Hint

How do fruit flies equalise X expression between the sexes?

Answer

Drosophila males double the transcription of their single X using the MSL complex. Without msl-2, the complex does not form, the male X is expressed at half the needed level, and males die. Females (XX) do not use the MSL complex (its assembly is blocked in females), so they are unaffected.

Q12. A person is heterozygous for a variant near gene G. RNA-seq reads at a silent SNP in G show 50 % from each allele, although total G expression is lower than in non-carriers. Does the variant more likely act in cis or in trans on G? What result would you expect for the other kind?

Hint

A cis-acting change affects only the copy on its own chromosome. What would that do to the ratio between the two alleles?

Answer

Trans. Both copies of G are turned down equally, as when a transcription factor is less abundant: the product reaches both alleles. A cis-acting variant (for example in an enhancer of G) would lower only the allele on the same chromosome, so the reads would become unequal (e.g. 20 : 80). Comparing the two alleles inside one person is the diploid version of the merodiploid cis–trans test.

Q13. A gene is expressed only from the paternal allele. An unaffected woman carries a loss-of-function allele. What is the risk that her child is affected? If her son inherits the allele, what is the risk for each of his children?

Hint

Transmission is ½ as usual. Then ask: from which parent does the child get the copy, and is that copy expressed?

Answer

Her children: 0. Any copy she passes on is maternal in the child, and the maternal copy is silent anyway. Her carrier son is unaffected for the same reason, but he passes the allele on with probability ½, as a paternal copy that is expressed: risk ½ for each of his children. The imprint is erased and re-set in each germ line according to the sex of the parent.
