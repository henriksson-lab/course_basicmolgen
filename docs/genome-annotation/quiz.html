Quiz: genome annotation

Short questions on assembly, gene finding, BLAST, genome browsers and gene families. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. Why does a genome assembled from 150-base reads break into many contigs, even at 50× coverage?

Hint

Think of a sequence that occurs at many places in the genome.

Answer

Repeats. A read that lies entirely inside a repeat (a transposable element, a duplicated gene, centromeric satellite DNA) matches every copy equally well, so the assembler cannot tell which copy it came from or which unique sequences flank which copy. More coverage does not help, because every read is still shorter than the repeat. Reads longer than the repeat (long-read sequencing) solve it.

Q2. How many reading frames does a double-stranded DNA sequence have, and why?

Hint

Codons are read in triplets, and either strand can be the template.

Answer

Six: three on each strand. On one strand you can start reading at the 1st, 2nd or 3rd base (the 4th gives the same frame as the 1st), and the complementary strand, read 5’→3’ in the other direction, gives three more.

Q3. In random DNA with equal base frequencies, what is the chance that a given codon is a stop codon? On average, how long is an open stretch between stops?

Hint

How many of the 64 codons are stop codons?

Answer

3 of the 64 codons are stops (TAA, TAG, TGA), so P(stop) = 3/64 ≈ 0.047. The average distance between stops is 64/3 ≈ 21 codons. An ORF of 300 codons is therefore very unlikely by chance: (61/64)300 ≈ 6 × 10−7.

Q4. Why is it much harder to find genes in the human genome by looking for long ORFs than in E. coli?

Hint

Compare the structure of a human gene with a bacterial one.

Answer

Human genes are split into exons separated by introns, so the coding sequence is broken into short pieces (an average exon is about 150 bp), and introns usually contain stop codons in all frames. Human genes are also far apart (about 6 per Mb, against about 1 per kb in bacteria), so most of the DNA is non-coding. Gene finders therefore need other evidence: splice sites (GT…AG), codon bias, RNA-seq and homology to known proteins.

Q5. Name three kinds of evidence that an annotation pipeline uses to decide that a stretch of human DNA is an exon.

Hint

Look at the tracks in the evidence figure in the lecture.

Answer

Any three of: RNA-seq reads mapping to it (it is transcribed), with split reads marking the exon junctions; protein homology (a known protein from another species aligns to it); conservation across species; sequence signals such as an open reading frame, splice sites (GT at the start and AG at the end of the flanking introns), start or stop codons, and typical codon usage.

Q6. A BLASTp search gives two hits: hit A with 35% identity over 140 of 147 query residues, E = 7 × 10−32; hit B with 60% identity over 20 residues, E = 0.9. Which one is more likely to be a real homolog?

Hint

The E value combines how good and how long the alignment is, given the database size.

Answer

Hit A. Its E value says that a hit this good would be expected by chance about 10−31 times, so it is almost certainly homologous, even though only 35% of positions are identical. (Human ζ-globin against β-globin gives numbers like hit A.) Hit B is more identical but over only 20 residues; an E value of 0.9 means you would expect about one such hit by chance in this database. Short, high-identity matches are often chance.

Q7. What is the difference between orthologs and paralogs? Classify: (a) human HBB and mouse Hbb-b1; (b) human HBB and human HBD; (c) human HBA1 and human HBB.

Hint

What event separated the two genes: a speciation or a gene duplication?

Answer

Orthologs are the “same” gene in different species, separated by a speciation event. Paralogs are related genes that arose by gene duplication, usually within one genome. (a) Orthologs. (b) Paralogs, from a recent duplication (93% identical proteins). (c) Paralogs, from an old duplication that separated the α-like and β-like globins (43% identical, now on chromosomes 16 and 11).

Q8. All five β-globin-like genes are on the minus strand of chromosome 11. HBE1 is at about 5.268 Mb and HBB at about 5.225 Mb. Which gene lies further upstream (5’) in the direction of transcription?

Hint

On the minus strand, genes are transcribed from higher to lower coordinates.

Answer

On the minus strand, transcription runs from higher to lower coordinates, so upstream means higher coordinates. HBE1 (5.268 Mb) is upstream of HBB (5.225 Mb). The cluster reads, 5’ to 3’: HBE1 – HBG2 – HBG1 – (HBBP1) – HBD – HBB, the order in which the genes are switched on during development (embryo, fetus, adult).

Q9. A genome browser shows a gene called HBBP1 in the β-globin cluster, with the biotype “pseudogene”. What is a pseudogene, and how does one usually arise?

Hint

Think about what can happen to an extra gene copy after a duplication.

Answer

A pseudogene is a sequence that looks like a gene (similar to a functional gene, sometimes with exons and introns) but cannot make a working product, because of stop codons, frameshifts or missing control regions. It usually arises after a gene duplication: with a second working copy around, mutations that destroy one copy are not removed by selection, and the copy decays. (Processed pseudogenes arise differently: an mRNA is reverse-transcribed and inserted back into the genome without introns.)

Q10. The Ensembl BioMart export in the R exercise has 20,107 protein-coding genes on 3,088 Mb of chromosome sequence. What is the average gene density? Chromosome 19 has 1,479 genes on 58.6 Mb. How many times denser is it than average?

Hint

Genes per Mb = genes / Mb.

Answer

Average: 20,107 / 3,088 ≈ 6.5 genes per Mb. Chromosome 19: 1,479 / 58.6 ≈ 25.2 genes per Mb, about 3.9 times the average. (Chromosome 13, at 2.8 per Mb, is the least dense autosome.)

Q11. A BLASTp hit has E = 4 × 10−6 in a database of 108 residues. You repeat the search against a database ten times larger, and the same alignment is found with the same bit score. What is its E value now? And what would E be for a hit with a bit score 10 bits higher, in the original database?

Hint

E ≈ m × n × 2−S: E is proportional to the database size n, and each extra bit halves it.

Answer

Ten times the database gives ten times the E value: 4 × 10−5. The alignment has not changed, but more chance hits of that quality are expected in a larger database. Ten more bits divide E by 210 ≈ 1,000: about 4 × 10−9.

Q12. Human HBA1 (α-globin) and mouse Hbb-b1 (β-globin) are in different species. Are they orthologs or paralogs?

Hint

Draw the gene tree: which event came first, the α/β duplication or the human/mouse speciation? At which kind of node did the two genes last split?

Answer

Paralogs. The α/β duplication is much older than the split between the human and mouse lineages, so the two genes last split at the duplication node. Being in different species does not make two genes orthologs; only a split at a speciation does. The ortholog of mouse Hbb-b1 in humans is HBB.
