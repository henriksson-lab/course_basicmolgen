Glossary

A running glossary of the genetics, database and statistics terms used in this course. Definitions are kept short, and each one links to the unit where the term is introduced.

Part 1: Mendelian genetics

Genes and genomes

Allele: one version of a gene. Alleles differ in DNA sequence. See Genetics as information.

C-value paradox: genome size does not follow gene number or organism complexity, because most of a large genome is non-coding DNA. See Genetics as information.

Dominant / recessive: if Aa has the same phenotype as AA, then A is dominant and a is recessive, for that phenotype. See Genetics as information.

Gene: a DNA segment that carries the information for one functional product, usually a protein. See Genetics as information.

Genotype: the alleles an individual carries at a locus, e.g. Aa. See Genetics as information.

Hemizygous: having only one copy of a gene, as males do for genes on the X chromosome. See Pedigree analysis.

Homozygous / heterozygous: carrying two identical alleles, or two different alleles, at a locus. See Genetics as information.

Locus: the position of a gene on a chromosome. See Genetics as information.

Model organism: a species that is easy to grow and cross, used because many of its genes are shared with other species. See Genetics as information.

Ortholog: genes in different species that descend from one gene in their common ancestor. See Genetics as information.

Paralog: genes related by a duplication within one genome. See Genetics as information.

Phenotype: an observable or measurable trait, from a disease to a protein band on a gel. See Genetics as information.

Wild type: the standard allele or phenotype in a lab population, written with a +, e.g. w+. See Genetics as information.

Mendel’s crosses

Branch-line (forked-line) method: solving a multi-gene cross one gene at a time, then multiplying the probabilities along each branch. See Mendel.

Dihybrid cross: a cross between individuals heterozygous for two genes (AaBb × AaBb). It gives 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 phenotypes when the genes are unlinked. See Mendel.

Law of independent assortment: alleles of different genes enter gametes independently, because homologue pairs orient independently at metaphase I. It holds for unlinked genes. See Mendel.

Law of segregation: the two alleles of a gene separate into different gametes, ½ each, because homologues separate in meiosis I. See Mendel.

Monohybrid cross: a cross between individuals heterozygous for one gene (Aa × Aa). It gives 1 : 2 : 1 genotypes and 3 : 1 phenotypes. See Mendel.

Punnett square: a grid of one parent’s gametes against the other parent’s gametes. Each cell is one equally likely fertilisation. See Mendel.

Reciprocal cross: the same cross done both ways round, to test whether it matters which parent carries the allele. See Mendel.

Testcross: a cross to a homozygous recessive individual. The offspring phenotypes show the other parent’s gametes directly. See Mendel.

True-breeding line: a line that gives only offspring like itself when self-fertilised, because it is homozygous. See Mendel.

Probability and the χ² test

Binomial distribution: the probability of k outcomes of one kind in n independent trials, (n choose k) pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. It is used for family composition. See Probability & the χ² test.

χ² goodness-of-fit test: a test of whether observed counts fit the counts expected from a hypothesised ratio, using χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E. See Probability & the χ² test.

Conditional probability: the probability of an event given that another event is known to have happened. For example, P(carrier | unaffected child of Aa × Aa) = 2/3. See Probability & the χ² test.

Critical value: the χ² value that is exceeded with probability 0.05 under H0 (3.84 for df = 1). See Probability & the χ² test.

Degrees of freedom (df): the number of counts that are free to vary once the total is fixed. It is k − 1 for k classes and a ratio given in advance. See Probability & the χ² test.

Fail to reject: the outcome when p ≥ 0.05. The data are consistent with H0, but this does not prove H0. See Probability & the χ² test.

Null hypothesis (H0): the hypothesis being tested, here a Mendelian ratio such as 3:1. Expected counts are computed assuming it is true. See Probability & the χ² test.

p-value: the probability, if H0 is true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. It is not the probability that H0 is true. See Probability & the χ² test.

Product rule: the probability that independent events all happen is the product of their probabilities, e.g. P(aabbcc) = (¼)³. See Probability & the χ² test.

Sum rule: the probability that one of several mutually exclusive events happens is the sum of their probabilities. See Probability & the χ² test.

Extensions to Mendel

Codominance: the heterozygote shows the phenotypes of both alleles in full, as in blood type AB (IAIB). See Extensions to Mendel.

Complementary genes: two genes that are both needed for a phenotype, giving a 9 : 7 F₂ ratio. See Extensions to Mendel.

Dominant negative: a mutant protein that interferes with the normal protein from the other allele, for example in a dimer. See Extensions to Mendel.

Duplicate genes: two redundant genes, where one working copy of either is enough, giving a 15 : 1 F₂ ratio. See Extensions to Mendel.

Epistasis: the genotype at one gene hides the effect of another gene. This changes the dihybrid 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio into ratios such as 9 : 3 : 4 or 9 : 7. See Extensions to Mendel.

Expressivity: how strongly a phenotype shows in the individuals who have it. See Extensions to Mendel.

Haploinsufficiency: one working copy of a gene does not make enough product for a normal phenotype, so a loss-of-function allele is dominant. See Extensions to Mendel.

Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote’s phenotype lies between those of the two homozygotes, so the F₂ phenotypes are 1 : 2 : 1, as in snapdragon flower colour. See Extensions to Mendel.

Multiple alleles: more than two alleles of a gene exist in a population, as in the ABO blood group. See Extensions to Mendel.

Penetrance: the fraction of individuals with a given genotype who show the expected phenotype at all. See Extensions to Mendel.

Pleiotropy: one gene or allele affecting several different traits. See Extensions to Mendel.

Recessive lethal allele: an allele that kills homozygotes. Among the survivors of a heterozygote cross this gives a 2 : 1 ratio, as for the yellow mouse allele AY. See Extensions to Mendel.

Pedigrees

Ascertainment bias: distortion of observed ratios caused by the way families are found, for example only through an affected child. See Pedigree analysis.

Consanguinity: a mating between related people, drawn as a double line. It raises the risk of recessive disease. See Pedigree analysis.

Male-to-male transmission: a trait passed from father to son. It rules out X-linked dominant inheritance and makes X-linked recessive inheritance unlikely. See Pedigree analysis.

Obligate carrier: an unaffected person who must be heterozygous because of where they are in the pedigree, for example a parent of a child with a recessive disease. See Pedigree analysis.

Pedigree: a family tree drawn with standard symbols that shows who is affected by a trait and how they are related. See Pedigree analysis.

Proband: the affected person through whom a family first comes to attention, marked with an arrow. See Pedigree analysis.

Databases

ClinVar: the NCBI database that links human variants to clinical interpretations. Each record has a classification and a review status. See Pedigree analysis.

HGVS notation: the standard naming of variants, e.g. c.1222C>T (coding DNA) and p.Arg408Trp (protein). See Pedigree analysis.

MIM number: the six-digit identifier of a gene or disease entry in OMIM. See Pedigree analysis.

Part 2: Chromosomes and mapping

Chromosome theory and sex linkage

Bivalent: a pair of synapsed homologues in prophase I, with four chromatids in all. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Chromosome theory of inheritance: genes are carried on chromosomes, and the behaviour of chromosomes in meiosis explains Mendel’s laws. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Criss-cross inheritance: an X-linked recessive trait passes from mother to sons and from father to daughters, so reciprocal crosses give different results. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Dosage compensation: mechanisms that equalise X-linked gene expression between XX and XY individuals, such as X inactivation. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Haplodiploidy: sex determination in which males develop from unfertilised eggs and are haploid, as in bees, ants and wasps. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Heterogametic sex: the sex with two different sex chromosomes (XY males, ZW females), which therefore makes two kinds of gamete. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Homologous chromosomes: the two copies of a chromosome in a diploid cell, one from each parent, with the same genes in the same order. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Nondisjunction: homologues (in meiosis I) or sister chromatids (in meiosis II) fail to separate, giving n + 1 and n − 1 gametes. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Reductional / equational division: meiosis I separates homologues (2n → n); meiosis II separates sister chromatids. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Sister chromatids: the two identical DNA copies of a replicated chromosome, joined at the centromere. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

SRY: the Y-linked gene (Yp11.2) encoding the testis-determining factor, which switches on male development in mammals. See Chromosome theory & sex linkage.

Linkage and recombination

Centimorgan (cM, map unit): the distance with on average one crossover per 100 chromatids. It is about 1% RF for short intervals. See Linkage & recombination.

Coupling (cis) / repulsion (trans): the linkage phase. In A B / a b the dominant alleles are on one homologue; in A b / a B there is one on each. See Linkage & recombination.

Crossing over / chiasma: reciprocal exchange between non-sister chromatids in prophase I. A chiasma is where it is seen under the microscope. See Linkage & recombination.

Four-strand stage: crossing over happens after replication, so one crossover makes only 2 of the 4 meiotic products recombinant. See Linkage & recombination.

Haldane map function: RF = ½(1 − e−2d). It assumes crossovers occur independently. See Linkage & recombination.

Kosambi map function: RF = ½ tanh(2d). It allows for interference. See Linkage & recombination.

Linkage: the tendency of genes close together on one chromosome to be inherited together, giving an excess of parental combinations (RF < 0.5). See Linkage & recombination.

Map function: a formula that converts between map distance and RF, correcting for hidden multiple crossovers. See Linkage & recombination.

Parental / recombinant class: offspring with the allele combinations the heterozygous parent received from its own parents, or with new combinations made by crossing over. See Linkage & recombination.

Recombination frequency (RF): recombinant offspring divided by all offspring. It lies between 0 and 0.5. See Linkage & recombination.

Three-point crosses

Coefficient of coincidence (CoC): observed double crossovers divided by expected double crossovers. See Three-point crosses.

Double crossover (DCO): a chromatid with one crossover in each of two adjacent intervals. It moves only the middle gene to the other homologue. See Three-point crosses.

Expected double crossovers: RFI × RFII × N, the number expected if crossovers in the two intervals are independent. See Three-point crosses.

Gene order (from a three-point cross): the middle gene is the one that differs between the double crossover classes and the parental classes. See Three-point crosses.

Interference (I): I = 1 − CoC. Positive interference means that one crossover makes another one nearby less likely. See Three-point crosses.

Negative interference: CoC > 1, i.e. more double crossovers than expected. It is rare; in a hand calculation it usually means the gene order is wrong. See Three-point crosses.

Reciprocal classes: two offspring classes with opposite alleles at every locus. They are made by the same crossover event, so they are similar in number. See Three-point crosses.

Single crossover (SCO): a chromatid with one crossover, in one of the two intervals of a three-point cross. See Three-point crosses.

Three-point testcross: a cross of an individual heterozygous for three linked genes to a triple recessive tester. The 8 offspring classes show the 8 gamete types. See Three-point crosses.

Human linkage and LOD scores

DNA marker: a site in the DNA where people differ (RFLP, microsatellite, SNP), used to tell a parent’s two homologues apart. It need not affect the phenotype. See Human linkage & LOD scores.

Exclusion mapping: using LOD ≤ −2 to rule out that a gene lies within a given distance of a marker. See Human linkage & LOD scores.

Informative meiosis: a meiosis in which the parent is heterozygous at both the disease locus and the marker, so each child can be scored as recombinant or non-recombinant. See Human linkage & LOD scores.

Likelihood: the probability of the observed data, read as a function of a parameter. For a phase-known family, L(θ) = θk(1 − θ)n−k. See Human linkage & LOD scores.

Linkage phase (in a pedigree): which marker allele lies on the same homologue as the disease allele. It can be known from the grandparents; otherwise both phases are averaged with probability ½ each. See Human linkage & LOD scores.

LOD score: log10 of the likelihood ratio L(θ)/L(0.5), comparing linkage at θ with no linkage. LODs from independent families add. See Human linkage & LOD scores.

LOD threshold: LOD ≥ 3 is accepted as linkage, LOD ≤ −2 excludes linkage at that θ, and values in between are inconclusive. See Human linkage & LOD scores.

Microsatellite: a short tandem repeat such as (CA)n, with many length alleles, so most people are heterozygous. It is typed by PCR. See Human linkage & LOD scores.

θ̂ (maximum likelihood estimate): the θ at which the LOD curve peaks. For phase-known families it equals total recombinants divided by total meioses. See Human linkage & LOD scores.

Part 3: DNA, mutation and gene function

DNA structure and replication

Antiparallel: the two strands of a double helix run in opposite directions, one 5′→3′ and the other 3′→5′. See DNA structure & replication.

Gene conversion: non-reciprocal transfer of sequence, caused by mismatch repair of heteroduplex DNA. It gives 3 : 1 instead of 2 : 2 in a tetrad. See DNA structure & replication.

Holliday junction: a point where strands cross between two homologous DNA helices during recombination. Cutting it gives a crossover or a non-crossover. See DNA structure & replication.

Reverse complement: the partner strand of a sequence, written 5′→3′: complement every base, then reverse the order. See DNA structure & replication.

Semiconservative replication: each daughter helix has one old and one new strand, as Meselson and Stahl showed. See DNA structure & replication.

Transformation: a heritable change caused by DNA taken up from outside the cell, as found by Griffith and by Avery. See DNA structure & replication.

Mutation and complementation

Ames test: a test for mutagens that counts his⁻ → his⁺ reversions in Salmonella, with liver extract added. See Mutation & complementation.

Complementation group: a set of mutations that fail to complement each other, which is one gene. See Mutation & complementation.

Complementation test: cross two recessive mutants. A wild-type F₁ means the mutations are in different genes; a mutant F₁ means they are in the same gene. See Mutation & complementation.

Fluctuation test: parallel cultures show jackpots (variance much larger than the mean). This shows that mutations arise at random, before selection. See Mutation & complementation.

Loss / gain of function: an allele that reduces or removes a gene’s activity, or one that raises it or adds a new activity. See Mutation & complementation.

Mutagen: an agent that raises the mutation rate above the spontaneous rate. See Mutation & complementation.

Silent, missense, nonsense, frameshift: a point mutation that keeps the amino acid, changes it, or creates a stop codon; or an insertion or deletion that shifts the reading frame. See Mutation & complementation.

Transition / transversion: a substitution within a base class (A↔︎G, C↔︎T), or one between a purine and a pyrimidine. See Mutation & complementation.

Gene expression

Alternative splicing: one gene gives several mRNAs by including or skipping exons. See Gene expression.

Intragenic suppression: a second mutation in the same gene restores function, for example a (−) frameshift next to a (+) frameshift. See Gene expression.

Isoform: one of several proteins made from the same gene. See Gene expression.

MANE Select: the one default transcript per gene agreed by NCBI and Ensembl. See Gene expression.

Nonsense-mediated decay (NMD): destruction of an mRNA with a premature stop codon. Such alleles therefore usually act as nulls. See Gene expression.

Reading frame: the grouping of a sequence into consecutive codons, starting from the start codon. See Gene expression.

Splice donor / acceptor: the GU at the 5′ end and the AG at the 3′ end of an intron. See Gene expression.

Part 4: Genome analysis

Digital analysis of DNA

Cloning vector: a small DNA molecule, such as a plasmid, with an origin of replication, a selectable marker and cloning sites. It is used to copy a DNA fragment in bacteria. See Digital analysis of DNA.

Coverage: the average number of sequencing reads covering each base: number of reads × read length / genome length. See Digital analysis of DNA.

Ct (threshold cycle): the PCR cycle at which the product crosses a detection threshold. With 10× more template it is crossed about 3.3 cycles earlier. See Digital analysis of DNA.

Gel electrophoresis: separation of DNA fragments by size in an agarose gel. The distance run falls roughly linearly with log(size). See Digital analysis of DNA.

PCR (polymerase chain reaction): repeated cycles of denaturing, annealing and extension that copy the DNA between two primers exponentially. See Digital analysis of DNA.

Primer-dimer: a short PCR artefact, made when two primers anneal to each other and are extended. See Digital analysis of DNA.

Restriction enzyme: a bacterial enzyme that cuts double-stranded DNA at a specific short recognition site, usually a palindrome. See Digital analysis of DNA.

RFLP: a restriction fragment length polymorphism, a variant that creates or destroys a restriction site and so changes fragment sizes. See Digital analysis of DNA.

Sanger sequencing: chain-termination sequencing with dye-labelled dideoxynucleotides. The products are sorted by length to read the bases. See Digital analysis of DNA.

Sticky end: a short single-stranded overhang left by a staggered cut. It can pair with any end cut by the same enzyme. See Digital analysis of DNA.

Genome annotation

Annotation: the evidence-based, versioned description of where the genes and other features lie in a genome and what they do. See Genome annotation.

BLAST: a program that finds local sequence similarity by seeding short word matches and extending them (blastn, blastp, blastx, tblastn). See Genome annotation.

Contig: a continuous stretch of sequence assembled from overlapping reads. See Genome annotation.

E-value: the number of alignments at least this good expected by chance in a database of this size. See Genome annotation.

Genome browser: a tool, such as UCSC or Ensembl, that shows annotation tracks along chromosome coordinates. See Genome annotation.

Open reading frame (ORF): a stretch from a start codon to the next in-frame stop codon. See Genome annotation.

Pseudogene: a gene copy that can no longer make a working product. See Genome annotation.

Genomic variation

Balanced polymorphism (heterozygote advantage): a polymorphism kept in a population because heterozygotes are the fittest, as for HbS where malaria is common. See Genomic variation.

Copy-number variant (CNV): a segment, from a kilobase to several megabases long, present in different numbers of copies in different people. See Genomic variation.

gnomAD: a large database of allele frequencies from exomes and genomes, grouped by genetic ancestry. See Genomic variation.

Haplotype: the set of alleles carried on one chromosome. See Genomic variation.

Indel: a small insertion or deletion of bases. See Genomic variation.

Lactase persistence: continued lactase production in adults. In Europeans it is mainly due to the dominant LCT −13910T allele (rs4988235). See Genomic variation.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD): a non-random association of alleles at nearby sites in a population. See Genomic variation.

rs number: the stable dbSNP identifier of a variant position. See Genomic variation.

SNP: a single-nucleotide variant common enough to count as a polymorphism. See Genomic variation.

Part 5: Chromosomes in detail

The eukaryotic chromosome

Centromere: the site on a chromosome where the kinetochore assembles. In humans it is Mb-long α-satellite arrays marked by the histone variant CENP-A. See The eukaryotic chromosome.

End-replication problem: the last primer on the lagging strand cannot be replaced by DNA, so linear chromosomes shorten at each replication. See The eukaryotic chromosome.

Heterochromatin: compact, gene-poor, late-replicating chromatin. It is built largely on satellite repeats (constitutive), or silenced in some cells only (facultative, e.g. the inactive X). See The eukaryotic chromosome.

Karyotype: the full chromosome set of a cell, ordered by size, centromere position and banding, and written e.g. 47,XY,+21. See The eukaryotic chromosome.

Nucleosome: 147 bp of DNA wrapped around an octamer of histones H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. There is about one per 200 bp of chromatin. See The eukaryotic chromosome.

Position-effect variegation: mosaic silencing of a gene that has been moved next to heterochromatin. The heterochromatin spreads into the gene in some cells but not others. See The eukaryotic chromosome.

Telomerase: a reverse transcriptase (TERT protein plus the TERC RNA template) that adds telomere repeats to chromosome ends. See The eukaryotic chromosome.

Telomere: the end of a linear chromosome. In vertebrates it is TTAGGG repeats with a 3′ overhang and the shelterin proteins, which hide the end from DNA repair. See The eukaryotic chromosome.

Yeast artificial chromosome (YAC): a vector with an origin (ARS), a centromere (CEN) and two telomeres. It carries 100 kb to over 1 Mb of foreign DNA in yeast. See The eukaryotic chromosome.

Rearrangements and aneuploidy

Allopolyploid / autopolyploid: a polyploid whose chromosome sets come from different species / from one species. See Rearrangements & aneuploidy.

Alternate / adjacent segregation: the ways the four chromosomes of a reciprocal translocation carrier can go to the poles. Only alternate segregation gives balanced gametes. See Rearrangements & aneuploidy.

Inversion loop: the loop that the homologues of an inversion heterozygote form so they can pair gene by gene. Crossovers inside the loop give unbalanced products. See Rearrangements & aneuploidy.

Maternal age effect: the steep rise in trisomy risk with the mother’s age. It is caused mainly by meiosis I errors in oocytes that have been arrested for a long time. See Rearrangements & aneuploidy.

Non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR): crossing over between mispaired low-copy repeats. It gives recurrent deletions and duplications of the segment between them. See Rearrangements & aneuploidy.

Paracentric / pericentric inversion: an inverted segment that excludes / includes the centromere. See Rearrangements & aneuploidy.

Philadelphia chromosome: der(22) of t(9;22). It carries the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene in chronic myeloid leukaemia. See Rearrangements & aneuploidy.

Pseudodominance: a recessive allele is expressed in a heterozygote because a deletion has removed the other copy. It is used for deletion mapping. See Rearrangements & aneuploidy.

Robertsonian translocation: a fusion of two acrocentric chromosomes near their centromeres. Carriers have 45 chromosomes. See Rearrangements & aneuploidy.

Bacterial genetics

Auxotroph: a mutant that cannot make a nutrient, so it grows only if the medium supplies it (e.g. Trp⁻). The wild type, which grows on minimal medium, is a prototroph. See Bacterial genetics.

Cotransduction frequency: the fraction of transductants, selected for one donor marker, that also received a second marker. The higher it is, the closer the genes. See Bacterial genetics.

F′ factor: an F plasmid that carries a piece of the bacterial chromosome. It makes the recipient a partial diploid (merodiploid). See Bacterial genetics.

Generalized transduction: transfer of a random piece of bacterial DNA from one cell to another inside a phage head (e.g. P1, about 100 kb). See Bacterial genetics.

Hfr strain: a bacterial strain with the F factor integrated into its chromosome, so that it transfers chromosomal genes in a fixed order from oriT. See Bacterial genetics.

Interrupted mating: breaking Hfr × F⁻ pairs apart at set times to measure each marker’s time of entry. This gives a map in minutes. See Bacterial genetics.

Map minute: the E. coli map unit. 100 minutes ≈ the whole 4.64 Mb chromosome, so 1 minute ≈ 46 kb. See Bacterial genetics.

Replica plating: printing the colonies of a master plate onto several test plates with a velvet, to score many colonies for growth at once. See Bacterial genetics.

Organelle inheritance

Endosymbiosis: the origin of mitochondria from an alphaproteobacterium, and of chloroplasts from a cyanobacterium, taken up by a host cell. Most of their genes were later transferred to the nucleus. See Organelle inheritance.

Heteroplasmy: both normal and mutant copies of the mitochondrial (or chloroplast) genome in one cell or person. The heteroplasmy level is the fraction of mutant copies. See Organelle inheritance.

Homoplasmy: all copies of the organelle genome in a cell or person are identical (a level of 0% or 100%). See Organelle inheritance.

Maternal inheritance: organelle genomes pass from the mother only, with the egg cytoplasm. Reciprocal crosses therefore differ, and fathers do not transmit. See Organelle inheritance.

MELAS: mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis and stroke-like episodes. It is usually caused by heteroplasmic m.3243A>G in MT-TL1 (tRNA-Leu). See Organelle inheritance.

Mitochondrial bottleneck: each oocyte is founded by a small number of mtDNA copies before they are amplified. Offspring heteroplasmy therefore varies widely around the mother’s level (variance h(1−h)/N). See Organelle inheritance.

Mitochondrial donation: transfer of the mother’s nuclear DNA (spindle or pronuclei) into an enucleated donor egg or zygote, so that the child has the donor’s mtDNA. See Organelle inheritance.

Replicative segregation: random sorting of organelle genomes between daughter cells at division. It changes heteroplasmy between tissues and over time, and causes variegation in plants. See Organelle inheritance.

Threshold effect: symptoms of a mtDNA disease appear only when the mutant fraction in a tissue exceeds a level that depends on the tissue and the variant, often 60–90%. See Organelle inheritance.

Uniparental inheritance: organelle DNA comes from only one parent. Which parent depends on the organism: maternal in humans, paternal cpDNA in many conifers, mt⁺ cpDNA in Chlamydomonas. See Organelle inheritance.

Part 6: Gene regulation and development

Prokaryotic regulation

Attenuation: control of transcription termination in a leader mRNA through alternative hairpins, as in the trp operon. See Prokaryotic regulation.

Cis-acting / trans-acting: affecting only the DNA molecule it is on (a site), or acting on all copies through a diffusible product. See Prokaryotic regulation.

Constitutive mutant: a mutant that expresses a gene with or without the inducer (e.g. lacI⁻, lacOᶜ). See Prokaryotic regulation.

Merodiploid: a bacterium with a second copy of part of its genome, usually on an F′ plasmid. It is used to test dominance and cis/trans action. See Prokaryotic regulation.

Super-repressor (lacIˢ): a repressor that cannot bind the inducer. It is uninducible and dominant in trans. See Prokaryotic regulation.

Eukaryotic regulation and epigenetics

Barr body: the condensed inactive X. A cell has (number of X − 1) of them. See Eukaryotic regulation & epigenetics.

Enhancer: a cis-acting DNA element that raises transcription, often far from the gene and tissue-specific. See Eukaryotic regulation & epigenetics.

Epigenetics: heritable changes in gene expression without a change in DNA sequence. See Eukaryotic regulation & epigenetics.

Genomic imprinting: expression of only the maternal or only the paternal allele. See Eukaryotic regulation & epigenetics.

Motif: the short, degenerate DNA sequence that a transcription factor binds. See Eukaryotic regulation & epigenetics.

Skewed X inactivation: use of one X in at least 80% of the cells of a tissue (80:20 or more). See Eukaryotic regulation & epigenetics.

X inactivation: random silencing of one X per cell in female mammals, inherited by the cell’s descendants and started by XIST. See Eukaryotic regulation & epigenetics.

RNA-mediated regulation

lncRNA: a non-coding transcript longer than 200 nt. See RNA-mediated regulation.

microRNA (miRNA): a ~22-nt RNA from a hairpin in the genome that represses mRNAs through seed pairing. See RNA-mediated regulation.

RNA interference (RNAi): silencing by siRNAs, made from double-stranded RNA, that guide cleavage of the matching mRNA. See RNA-mediated regulation.

Seed: nucleotides 2–8 of a miRNA. See RNA-mediated regulation.

siRNA: a ~21-nt RNA made by Dicer from long double-stranded RNA. It pairs perfectly with one target. See RNA-mediated regulation.

Development and model organisms

Balancer chromosome: a chromosome with inversions, a dominant marker and a recessive lethal. It is used to keep mutations in stocks. See Development & model organisms.

Forward / reverse genetics: from phenotype to gene, or from gene to phenotype. See Development & model organisms.

Hox genes / colinearity: homeobox genes that give segments their identity. Their order on the chromosome matches the order of the body parts they specify. See Development & model organisms.

Maternal-effect gene: a gene for which the embryo’s phenotype depends on the mother’s genotype. See Development & model organisms.

Part 7: Cancer, populations and complex traits

Cancer genetics

Driver mutation: a somatic mutation that gives a cell a growth advantage and is selected during tumour evolution. Contrast with a passenger mutation. See Cancer genetics.

Loss of heterozygosity (LOH): loss of one allele of a heterozygous locus in a tumour, so nearby markers look homozygous in tumour DNA. See Cancer genetics.

Mutator phenotype: a raised mutation rate in a cell lineage, usually from loss of a DNA-repair gene. See Cancer genetics.

Oncogene: a mutated, overactive form of a gene that promotes cell division (the normal form is a proto-oncogene). It is a gain of function, so one mutant allele is enough: dominant at the cell level. See Cancer genetics.

Tumour suppressor gene: a gene whose product restrains division or repairs DNA. A cell becomes abnormal only when both alleles are lost: recessive at the cell level. See Cancer genetics.

Two-hit hypothesis: Knudson’s model, in which a tumour arises when both alleles of a tumour suppressor are inactivated in one cell. In hereditary cases the first hit is inherited. See Cancer genetics.

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

Allele frequency: the fraction of gene copies in a population that are a given allele; p = f(AA) + ½ f(Aa). See Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

de Finetti triangle: a triangle in which each population is a point (q across, heterozygote frequency up). Populations in HWE lie on a parabola. See Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE): genotype frequencies p², 2pq and q². They are reached after one generation of random mating and stay constant without drift, selection, mutation or migration. See Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

Identical by descent: two alleles that are copies of the same ancestral allele. See Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

Inbreeding coefficient (F): the probability that a person’s two alleles are identical by descent. It is estimated as 1 − Hobs/Hexp. See Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

Wahlund effect: a deficit of heterozygotes in a sample pooled from populations with different allele frequencies. See Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

Drift, selection, mutation, migration

Effective population size (Ne): the size of an ideal population that drifts as fast as the real one. It is usually much smaller than the census size. See Drift, selection, mutation, migration.

Fixation: an allele reaching frequency 1. For a neutral allele, the probability of fixation equals its current frequency. See Drift, selection, mutation, migration.

Founder effect: changes in allele frequencies because a new population starts from few individuals. A bottleneck has the same effect. See Drift, selection, mutation, migration.

Gene flow (migration): movement of alleles between populations; Δp = m(pM − p). See Drift, selection, mutation, migration.

Genetic drift: random change in allele frequency due to the sampling of gene copies each generation. It is strongest in small populations. See Drift, selection, mutation, migration.

Heterozygote advantage (overdominance): heterozygotes are fitter than both homozygotes, which keeps the allele at a stable intermediate frequency, as with HbS and malaria. See Drift, selection, mutation, migration.

Mutation–selection balance: an equilibrium in which new mutations replace the alleles removed by selection. For a recessive allele, q̂ ≈ √(μ/s). See Drift, selection, mutation, migration.

Relative fitness (w): the relative number of offspring left by a genotype. s is the selection coefficient and h the dominance of the fitness effect. See Drift, selection, mutation, migration.

Wright–Fisher model: a model of drift in which the next generation’s 2N gene copies are drawn at random from the current one. See Drift, selection, mutation, migration.

Complex traits, heritability and GWAS

Broad-sense heritability (H²): VG/VP, the fraction of phenotypic variance due to all genetic differences. See Complex traits, heritability & GWAS.

Complex (quantitative) trait: a continuously varying trait influenced by many loci and by the environment. See Complex traits, heritability & GWAS.

Genome-wide association study (GWAS): a test of association between a trait and each of very many SNPs in unrelated people. The significance threshold is 5 × 10⁻⁸. See Complex traits, heritability & GWAS.

Manhattan plot: −log10 p of every SNP plotted against its position in the genome. Associated loci appear as towers. See Complex traits, heritability & GWAS.

Missing heritability: the gap between heritability estimated from families and the variance explained by GWAS hits. See Complex traits, heritability & GWAS.

Narrow-sense heritability (h²): VA/VP, the fraction of phenotypic variance due to additive genetic variance. It equals the slope of offspring on midparent. See Complex traits, heritability & GWAS.

Polygenic score: a person’s sum of risk alleles, each weighted by its GWAS effect size. See Complex traits, heritability & GWAS.

Quantitative trait locus (QTL): a locus that affects a quantitative trait, mapped by linkage in crosses. See Complex traits, heritability & GWAS.

Regression towards the mean: offspring of extreme parents are on average less extreme, by the factor h². See Complex traits, heritability & GWAS.

Ethics

Autonomy: respect for a person’s own informed, voluntary choices. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Beneficence / non-maleficence: the duty to do good, and the duty to avoid harm. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Direct-to-consumer (DTC) test: a genetic test sold directly to the public, without a doctor. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Eugenics: the attempt to “improve” a population by controlling reproduction. Galton coined the word in 1883. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Gene drive: an allele that copies itself onto the homologous chromosome, so that it reaches nearly all gametes instead of half. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Germline editing: changing the DNA of an embryo or gametes so that the change is inherited. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Incidental / secondary finding: a result unrelated to the reason for testing. An incidental finding turns up by chance; a secondary finding is looked for on purpose in actionable genes. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Informed consent: voluntary agreement to a test or to research, given after understanding what it involves. It can be withdrawn. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Investigative genetic genealogy: identifying the donor of crime-scene DNA through distant relatives in genealogy databases. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Justice (bioethics): fair distribution of the benefits, risks and costs of care and research. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

NIPT: non-invasive prenatal testing, a screening test on cell-free fetal DNA in the mother’s blood. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Predictive testing: testing a healthy person for a variant that forecasts later disease, e.g. Huntington disease. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Re-identification: recovering a person’s identity from data with the names removed, e.g. surnames from Y-STRs. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.

Right not to know: a person’s right to decline learning a genetic result about themselves. See Ethics of genetic knowledge.
