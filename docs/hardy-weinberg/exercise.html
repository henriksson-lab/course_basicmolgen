Exercise: Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • compute allele and genotype frequencies from counts;
  • derive the Hardy–Weinberg proportions from random mating, and state the assumptions;
  • test a sample for HWE with a χ² test (df = 1), by hand;
  • compute carrier frequencies from disease incidence, for autosomal and X-linked traits;
  • explain how inbreeding (F) and population structure produce a heterozygote deficit;
  • read real genotype counts in Ensembl (1000 Genomes) and homozygote counts in gnomAD.

Do the pen-and-paper part first. The demo and R come after.

Background

Frequencies. With counts nAA, nAa, naa and N people:

\[ p = \frac{2n_{AA} + n_{Aa}}{2N}, \qquad q = 1 - p. \]

Hardy–Weinberg proportions (random mating, large population, no selection, mutation or migration): AA = p², Aa = 2pq, aa = q². Reached after one generation of random mating; p stays constant.

χ² test for HWE. Expected counts Np², 2Npq, Nq² from the estimated p. χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E with df = 1 (3 classes − 1 − 1 estimated parameter). Reject HWE at 5% if χ² > 3.84. Expected counts below about 5 make the test unreliable.

Carriers. If a recessive disease has incidence q², carriers = 2pq ≈ 2q when q is small. X-linked: affected males = q, affected females = q², carrier females = 2pq.

Inbreeding. AA = p² + Fpq, Aa = 2pq(1 − F), aa = q² + Fpq. Observed heterozygosity gives F = 1 − Hobs/Hexp. Child of first cousins: F = 1/16.

By hand

B1. (counting) At the bitter-taste receptor SNP rs713598 in TAS2R38, 103 Han Chinese from Beijing (1000 Genomes CHB) have 13 CC, 41 CG and 49 GG. (a) What are the genotype frequencies and the allele frequencies? (b) What counts does HWE predict? (c) Compute χ² and decide whether to reject HWE at the 5% level.

B2. (another test) At the TCF7L2 SNP rs7903146, the 108 Yoruba from Ibadan (YRI) have 60 CC, 44 CT and 4 TT. Test HWE. Which class contributes most to χ², and why should you be careful with it?

B3. (one generation is enough) A new population is founded by 60 AA and 40 aa individuals, and no heterozygotes. (a) What is p? Is the founding population in HWE? (b) What are the genotype frequencies after one generation of random mating? After two?

B4. (dominant phenotype) In a population, 19% of people show a dominant phenotype (A_), and the rest are aa. (a) Assuming HWE, what are q and p? (b) What fraction of the people with the dominant phenotype are heterozygous?

B5. (carriers from incidence) A recessive disease affects 1 in 40 000 newborns. (a) What is the carrier frequency, assuming HWE? Write it as “1 in …”. (b) Two people from this population, with no family history, have a child. What is the risk that the child is affected? Check that your answer matches the incidence. (c) A woman whose brother has the disease (both parents unaffected) has a child with a man from the population. What is the risk for the child? (Recall the pedigrees unit.)

B6. (X-linked) Red–green colour blindness is X-linked recessive. In a population, 8% of men are colour-blind. (a) What is q? (b) What fraction of women are colour-blind, and what fraction are carriers? (c) A colour-blind man has a daughter with a woman of unknown genotype from this population. What is the probability that a son of that daughter is colour-blind?

B7. (inbreeding) In a plant population with p = 0.6, the observed heterozygote frequency is 0.36. (a) What does HWE predict? What is F? (b) What are the three genotype frequencies? (c) For a rare recessive allele with q = 0.002, compare f(aa) in a child of unrelated parents and in a child of first cousins (F = 1/16).

B8. (population structure) Two equally large villages are each in HWE: in village 1, p = 0.8; in village 2, p = 0.2. (a) What is the heterozygote frequency in each village, and in a sample that pools both villages equally? (b) What does HWE predict for the pooled sample? What is the apparent F? (c) After one generation in which everyone mates at random across both villages, what are the genotype frequencies?

Explore

The demo shows a de Finetti triangle: every population is a point, at horizontal position q and height f(Aa). The green curve is HWE. Drag the point, or use the p and F sliders and the presets. The bars compare the observed counts for a sample of N people with the HWE expectation, and the panel shows the χ² test.

E1. Excess heterozygotes, and the role of N. Press excess heterozygotes (genotype frequencies 0.2, 0.7, 0.1). (a) What are p, Hobs, Hexp and F? Is the point above or below the parabola? (b) Move the N slider from 20 up to 1000 and note χ² and p each time. The frequencies do not change: why does χ² grow? At which N do you first reject HWE at the 5% level? (c) Give one biological reason for an excess of heterozygotes.

E2. Inbreeding. Press inbreeding F = 0.25. (a) Check the three genotype frequencies against p² + Fpq, 2pq(1 − F), q² + Fpq. (b) With N = 20, 50, 100 and 200, does the χ² test detect the deficit? (c) Press fully inbred F = 1. What kind of population is this, and why does the point lie on the base? (d) Press Mendel F2 1:2:1. Where is the point? What does that tell you about one generation of random mating?

E3. A rare recessive allele. Press rare allele q = 0.1. (a) Read the frequency of aa. (b) Set F = 0.25 with the slider. By what factor does the frequency of aa increase? By what factor do the heterozygotes decrease? (c) Repeat by hand for F = 1/16 at q = 0.1 and at q = 0.01. What does this say about rare recessive diseases in children of related parents?

E4. A classic sample: the MN blood group. The demo below starts from a classic textbook sample of 6129 people with 1787 MM, 3039 MN and 1303 NN (M plays the role of A). (a) Compute p and the HWE expected counts by hand, then compare with the demo. (b) What are χ² and the p-value? Is the sample in HWE? (c) Where does the point sit relative to the parabola?

Solutions: Explore

E1. (a) p = 0.2 + 0.7/2 = 0.55, q = 0.45. Hobs = 0.70, Hexp = 2 × 0.55 × 0.45 = 0.495, F = 1 − 0.70/0.495 = −0.41. The point lies above the parabola. (b)

N counts (AA, Aa, aa) χ² p
20 4, 14, 2 3.43 0.064
50 10, 35, 5 8.58 0.0034
100 20, 70, 10 17.2 3.5 × 10⁻⁵
200 40, 140, 20 34.3 4.7 × 10⁻⁹
1000 200, 700, 100 172 3.5 × 10⁻³⁹

χ² grows in proportion to N, because each (O − E)² grows as N² while E grows only as N. (For the HWE test, χ² is exactly N·F²: 200 × 0.414² = 34.3.) HWE is first rejected at N = 50. A small sample can only detect large departures. (c) For example heterozygote advantage acting on the sampled adults (HbS and malaria, next unit); a sample of the offspring of matings between two populations with different allele frequencies; or genotyping errors that call homozygotes as heterozygotes.

E2. (a) p = q = 0.5, pq = 0.25: AA = 0.25 + 0.25 × 0.25 = 0.3125, Aa = 0.5 × 0.75 = 0.375, aa = 0.3125. The missing heterozygotes are split equally between the homozygotes; p does not change. (b)

N counts χ² p
20 6, 8, 6 0.80 0.37
50 16, 19, 15 2.87 0.090
100 31, 38, 31 5.76 0.016
200 63, 75, 62 12.5 0.0004

Not at N = 20 or 50; yes from N = 100 (χ² ≈ N·F² = N/16). (c) F = 1 gives 0.5 AA, 0 Aa, 0.5 aa: no heterozygotes, as in a set of fully inbred lines (a plant that has selfed for many generations, or Mendel’s true-breeding parents mixed together). All points with f(Aa) = 0 lie on the base. (d) Exactly on the parabola, at its top (q = 0.5, f(Aa) = 0.5), χ² = 0. An F2 is one generation of random union of gametes from Aa parents, so it is in HWE. One generation of random mating reaches HWE from any starting genotype frequencies.

E3. (a) f(aa) = q² = 0.01. (b) With F = 0.25: 0.01 + 0.25 × 0.09 = 0.0325, 3.25 times as many; heterozygotes fall from 0.18 to 0.135, a factor 1 − F = 0.75. (c) F = 1/16, q = 0.1: 0.01 + 0.09/16 = 0.0156, a factor of 1.56. q = 0.01: 0.0001 + 0.0099/16 = 0.00072, a factor of 7.2. The rarer the allele, the larger the relative increase, because almost all copies are in heterozygotes and the extra homozygotes (Fpq) dwarf q². This is why many patients with very rare recessive diseases have related parents.

E4. (a) p(M) = (2 × 1787 + 3039)/(2 × 6129) = 6613/12258 = 0.5395, q = 0.4605. Expected: 1783.8 MM, 3045.4 MN, 1299.8 NN. (b) χ² = 0.027, df = 1, p = 0.87: no evidence against HWE. (c) Practically on the parabola: Hobs = 0.496, Hexp = 0.497, F = 0.002.

Find it

F1. Genotype counts in Ensembl. Open the sickle-cell variant rs334 in Ensembl and go to Population genetics in the left menu. In the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 table, find the populations YRI (Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria) and GWD (Gambian in Western Division). Read the allele frequencies, and the genotype counts or frequencies (the genotype columns, or click the population). The same data in machine-readable form: rest.ensembl.org/variation/human/rs334?population_genotypes=1. (a) How many people in YRI and in GWD carry each genotype? (The two alleles are T and A on the forward strand; A is HbS.) (b) Test HWE in YRI by hand. Comment on the number of HbS homozygotes. (c) Which populations outside Africa carry the A allele at all?

F2. A common stop codon. Search Ensembl for rs1815739 in ACTN3. What is its most severe consequence, and what does it do to the protein (R577X)? In the Population genetics view, read the genotype counts in GBR (British). Are the counts consistent with HWE? About what fraction of British people are homozygous for the stop allele?

F3. Homozygotes in gnomAD. Open rs334 in gnomAD v4. In the Genetic Ancestry Group Frequencies table, read the row African/African American, genomes: allele count, allele number and number of homozygotes. (a) Compute the allele frequency q and the number of people (allele number / 2). (b) How many homozygotes does HWE predict? Compare with the number observed. Explain the difference.

F4. Carrier frequency of cystic fibrosis. Open CFTR F508del, rs113993960 in gnomAD. Read the allele frequency in the European (non-Finnish) and the European (Finnish) groups (genomes). (a) Estimate the carrier frequency of F508del in each group (≈ 2q), as “1 in …”. (b) The pedigrees unit used a carrier frequency of 1/50 for PKU. How does the CF carrier frequency in non-Finnish Europeans compare? Why is the carrier frequency for all CF alleles higher than for F508del alone?

Solutions: Find it

Checked on 20 November 2026 via the Ensembl REST API (release 116; 1000 Genomes phase 3 is a fixed data set) and the gnomAD v4 API. gnomAD counts may change slightly in later releases; the conclusions will not.

F1. Click path: ensembl.org → search rs334 → variant page → Population genetics → 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 table. (a) YRI: 78 T|T, 30 A|T, 0 A|A (108 people). GWD: 87 T|T, 26 A|T, 0 A|A (113 people). (b) q(A) = 30/216 = 0.139. Expected: 108 × 0.861² = 80.1 AA, 108 × 2 × 0.861 × 0.139 = 25.8 AS, 108 × 0.139² = 2.1 SS. χ² = 0.05 + 0.67 + 2.08 = 2.8 (p = 0.09): not significant, but the expected SS count is below 5, so the test is weak. The direction is clear: two SS people are expected and none is seen. People with sickle-cell disease are missing from this sample of healthy adults. (c) Outside the African populations (YRI, GWD, ESN, LWK, MSL), HbS heterozygotes appear only in populations with recent African ancestry: ACB (African Caribbean in Barbados, 9), ASW (African ancestry in the southwestern US, 2), PUR (Puerto Rican, 3) and CLM (Colombian, 2). No European, East Asian or South Asian sample in 1000 Genomes carries it. (Some Mediterranean, Middle Eastern and Indian populations not sampled here do carry HbS.)

F2. rs1815739: most severe consequence stop_gained. The T allele changes codon 577 from arginine (CGA) to a stop codon (TGA): R577X, a truncated α-actinin-3 that is not made in fast muscle fibres. GBR: 26 C|C, 45 C|T, 20 T|T (91 people). p = 0.533; expected 25.9, 45.3, 19.8; χ² = 0.004 (p = 0.95): in excellent HWE. About 22% (20/91) of the British sample is homozygous XX and makes no α-actinin-3, without disease. A common homozygous loss-of-function genotype is a good reminder that not all “knockouts” are harmful.

F3. gnomAD v4 genomes, African/African American: allele count 1810, allele number 41 554, homozygotes 9. (a) q = 1810/41 554 = 0.044; people ≈ 41 554/2 = 20 777. (b) HWE predicts 20 777 × 0.044² ≈ 39 homozygotes; gnomAD has 9, about a quarter. gnomAD aggregates studies of adults, and people with severe childhood disease are mostly excluded; sickle-cell disease (SS) also shortens life. Selection and ascertainment remove SS from the sample, so HWE fails for this genotype even though random mating produced it at conception.

F4. gnomAD v4 genomes: non-Finnish European allele frequency 0.0143 (972/67 954); Finnish 0.0015 (16/10 612). (a) Non-Finnish Europeans: 2pq = 0.028, about 1 in 35. Finns: 0.0030, about 1 in 330. (b) F508del alone gives about 1 in 35, already more common than the PKU carriers (1/50). CF is caused by hundreds of different CFTR alleles. A person is a carrier if they carry any of them, so the carrier frequency for CF as a whole is higher (about 1 in 25–30 in many north-west European populations) than for F508del alone. The low value in Finland is a population-history effect; see Drift, selection and the Finnish disease heritage. (gnomAD also lists 1 F508del homozygote among the non-Finnish Europeans, where HWE predicts about 7: people with CF are under-represented in gnomAD, as for sickle-cell disease.)

Solutions

Solutions: By hand

B1. (a) Genotypes: 13/103 = 0.126, 41/103 = 0.398, 49/103 = 0.476. p(C) = (26 + 41)/206 = 0.325, q(G) = 0.675. (b) Expected: 103 × 0.325² = 10.9 CC, 103 × 2 × 0.325 × 0.675 = 45.2 CG, 103 × 0.675² = 46.9 GG. (c) χ² = (13 − 10.9)²/10.9 + (41 − 45.2)²/45.2 + (49 − 46.9)²/46.9 = 0.41 + 0.39 + 0.09 = 0.89. df = 1, critical value 3.84: do not reject HWE (p = 0.35).

B2. p = (120 + 44)/216 = 0.759, q = 0.241. Expected 62.3, 39.5, 6.3. χ² = 0.08 + 0.52 + 0.82 = 1.42 (p = 0.23): consistent with HWE. The largest contribution comes from TT, where the expected count (6.3) is small: a difference of 2 people gives 0.8. With expected counts near or below 5, single individuals move χ² a lot and the χ² distribution fits poorly.

B3. (a) p = 0.6 (all alleles of the 60 AA, out of 200). Not in HWE: HWE predicts 48% heterozygotes and there are none. (b) After one generation of random mating: 0.36 AA, 0.48 Aa, 0.16 aa. After two generations: the same. p stays 0.6, and one generation is enough to reach HWE.

B4. (a) aa = 0.81 = q², so q = 0.9 and p = 0.1. (b) AA = 0.01, Aa = 2 × 0.1 × 0.9 = 0.18. Among the 0.19 with the dominant phenotype, 0.18/0.19 = 0.95 are heterozygous. A rare dominant phenotype is almost always carried by heterozygotes.

B5. (a) q = √(1/40 000) = 1/200 = 0.005. Carriers 2pq = 2 × 0.995 × 0.005 = 0.00995 ≈ 1 in 100. (b) 1/100 × 1/100 × 1/4 = 1/40 000, the incidence, as it should be. (c) She is an unaffected sibling of a patient, so a carrier with probability 2/3. Risk = 2/3 × 1/100 × 1/4 = 1/600.

B6. (a) q = 0.08 (affected males = q). (b) Colour-blind women q² = 0.0064 (0.64%); carriers 2pq = 2 × 0.92 × 0.08 = 0.147 (about 15%). (c) The daughter gets her father’s Xa, so she is at least a carrier. From her mother she gets Xa with probability q = 0.08. Her son gets Xa from her with probability ½ if she is XAXa and 1 if she is XaXa: 0.92 × ½ + 0.08 × 1 = 0.46 + 0.08 = 0.54.

B7. (a) HWE: 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.48. F = 1 − 0.36/0.48 = 0.25. (b) AA = 0.36 + 0.25 × 0.24 = 0.42, Aa = 0.36, aa = 0.16 + 0.06 = 0.22. (Check: 0.42 + 0.36 + 0.22 = 1, and p = 0.42 + 0.18 = 0.6, unchanged.) (c) Unrelated parents: q² = 0.000004 (1 in 250 000). First cousins: 0.000004 + 0.998 × 0.002/16 = 0.000004 + 0.000125 = 0.000129 (about 1 in 7800), about 32 times higher.

B8. (a) Each village: 2 × 0.8 × 0.2 = 0.32. Pooled: also 0.32. (b) Pooled p = 0.5, so HWE predicts 0.50. Apparent F = 1 − 0.32/0.50 = 0.36: a heterozygote deficit from population structure, the Wahlund effect. (c) With random mating across villages, p = 0.5 for everyone: 0.25, 0.50, 0.25. The deficit disappears after one generation.

Where this leads

Today p stood still. In Drift, selection, mutation, migration we let it move: by chance in small populations, and by selection, including the heterozygote advantage that keeps HbS common where malaria is. In Complex traits, HWE is a quality filter for GWAS data.
