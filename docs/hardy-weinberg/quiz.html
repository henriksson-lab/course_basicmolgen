Quiz: Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

Short questions on allele and genotype frequencies, the Hardy–Weinberg principle and inbreeding. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. In a sample of 200 people, 98 are AA, 84 are Aa and 18 are aa. What are the genotype frequencies and the allele frequencies?

Hint

Genotype frequencies count people. Allele frequencies count gene copies: two per person.

Answer

Genotypes: 98/200 = 0.49, 84/200 = 0.42, 18/200 = 0.09. Alleles: p = (2 × 98 + 84)/400 = 280/400 = 0.7; q = 0.3. (Shortcut: p = 0.49 + 0.42/2 = 0.70.) These genotype frequencies are exactly 0.7², 2 × 0.7 × 0.3 and 0.3²: the sample is in HWE.

Q2. A student says: “A is dominant, so over the generations A will spread until everyone has the dominant phenotype.” What is wrong?

Hint

What happens to p after one generation of random mating?

Answer

Dominance decides how a genotype looks, not how often an allele is passed on. Under random mating, p in the next generation is p² + pq = p: unchanged. An allele only spreads if something else acts on it, such as selection or drift. Huntington disease is dominant and rare; blood group O is recessive and common in many populations.

Q3. List the assumptions of the Hardy–Weinberg principle. Which of them, if broken, changes genotype frequencies but not allele frequencies?

Hint

Think about what inbreeding does to heterozygotes and homozygotes.

Answer

Random mating, a very large population (no drift), no selection, no mutation, no migration (and a single population). Non-random mating, such as inbreeding, shifts genotypes from heterozygotes to homozygotes but leaves p unchanged. Pooling different populations (Wahlund effect) does the same. Drift, selection, mutation and migration change p itself.

Q4. Why does the χ² test for HWE have 1 degree of freedom, although there are three genotype classes?

Hint

In Mendel’s crosses, where did the expected ratio come from?

Answer

We lose one degree of freedom because the three counts must add up to N, and another because the expected frequencies use p, which we estimated from the same data. 3 − 1 − 1 = 1. In a Mendelian cross the ratio came from theory, so no parameter was estimated and df = classes − 1. The 5% critical value for df = 1 is 3.84.

Q5. Phenylketonuria (autosomal recessive) affects about 1 in 10 000 newborns. Assuming HWE, what fraction of people are carriers?

Hint

Incidence = q². Then carriers = 2pq.

Answer

q = √(1/10 000) = 0.01, p = 0.99. Carriers 2pq = 2 × 0.99 × 0.01 = 0.0198 ≈ 1/50. This is the carrier frequency used in the pedigrees unit. For a rare allele, carriers ≈ 2q.

Q6. About 1 in 5000 boys is born with haemophilia A (X-linked recessive). Estimate the frequency of affected girls and of carrier women.

Hint

Males have one X. What is the frequency of affected males in terms of q?

Answer

Affected males = q = 1/5000 = 0.0002. Affected females = q² = 4 × 10−8, about 1 in 25 million. Carrier females = 2pq ≈ 2 × 0.0002 = 0.0004, about 1 in 2500. (In reality many cases are new mutations, which this simple calculation ignores.)

Q7. In a de Finetti triangle, a population lies below the HWE parabola. Give two possible explanations. What could put it above the parabola?

Hint

Below means too few heterozygotes.

Answer

Below (heterozygote deficit): inbreeding (F > 0), or population structure: a sample pooled from populations with different allele frequencies (Wahlund effect). Selection against heterozygotes or genotyping errors that miss heterozygotes also do it. Above (excess): heterozygote advantage acting on the sampled individuals, the offspring of a cross between two different populations, or genotyping errors that call too many heterozygotes.

Q8. What does an inbreeding coefficient F = 1/16 mean for a child of first cousins? How does it change the frequency of aa for an allele with q = 0.01?

Hint

f(aa) = q² + Fpq.

Answer

At any locus, the child’s two alleles are copies of the same ancestral allele (identical by descent) with probability 1/16. f(aa) = 0.0001 + 0.99 × 0.01/16 = 0.0001 + 0.00062 = 0.00072, about 7 times higher than under random mating. The rarer the allele, the larger the relative increase.

Q9. In 1000 Genomes, the Yoruba sample has 78 AA, 30 AS and 0 SS people at the sickle-cell SNP. HWE predicts about 2 SS. A much larger sample (gnomAD) shows the same deficit. Why is the sickle-cell locus not in HWE among adults?

Hint

HWE holds at conception. What happens between conception and the time a person is sampled?

Answer

Selection: SS people have sickle-cell disease, which shortens life (especially without treatment), and people with severe disease are rarely included in studies of healthy adults. So SS genotypes are missing from adult samples, although random mating produced them at conception in HWE proportions.

Q10. Two equally large populations are each in HWE, one with p = 0.9 and one with p = 0.1. You sample both and pool the data. What heterozygote frequency do you observe, and what does HWE predict for the pooled p?

Hint

Compute 2pq in each population and average. Then compute the pooled p.

Answer

Each population has 2 × 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.18 heterozygotes, so the pooled sample has 0.18. Pooled p = 0.5, so HWE predicts 0.50. Apparent F = 1 − 0.18/0.50 = 0.64, a large heterozygote deficit without any inbreeding: the Wahlund effect.

Q11. A trait is recessive with complete dominance, and 4% of a population show it. Assuming HWE, what is q? What fraction of the people with the dominant phenotype are carriers (Aa)? Why can you not test HWE with these data?

Hint

Only aa can be told apart, and f(aa) = q² under HWE.

Answer

q = √0.04 = 0.2, p = 0.8. AA = 0.64, Aa = 2 × 0.8 × 0.2 = 0.32, so among the 96% with the dominant phenotype, 0.32/0.96 = one third are carriers. HWE cannot be tested: there are only two visible classes, and estimating q uses up the only degree of freedom (df = 2 − 1 − 1 = 0). HWE is an assumption here, not a result.
