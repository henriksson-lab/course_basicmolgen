Quiz: rearrangements & aneuploidy

Short questions on deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations, aneuploidy and polyploidy. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. Which of these rearrangements are balanced, and which are unbalanced: deletion, duplication, paracentric inversion, pericentric inversion, reciprocal translocation, Robertsonian translocation?

Hint

Does the carrier have exactly two copies of every part of the genome (apart from repeats)?

Answer

Unbalanced: deletion (too little) and duplication (too much). Balanced: both kinds of inversion, reciprocal translocations, and Robertsonian translocations (only the repetitive short arms of two acrocentrics are lost, and copies of them remain on the other acrocentrics). Balanced carriers are usually healthy, but they make unbalanced gametes.

Q2. A Drosophila female heterozygous for a deletion is crossed to a male homozygous for a recessive mutation m. Half of the offspring show the mutant phenotype. What does that tell you about m, and what is this phenomenon called?

Hint

Which offspring have only one copy of the region, and which allele is it?

Answer

m lies inside the deleted region. The offspring that got the deletion chromosome from their mother have only the father’s m allele in that region, so the recessive phenotype shows: pseudodominance. Overlapping deletions that do or do not uncover m map it to a small interval: deletion mapping.

Q3. Why do 22q11.2 deletions in unrelated patients usually have almost the same breakpoints?

Hint

What sits at the ends of the deleted segment?

Answer

The segment is flanked by low-copy repeats (segmental duplications). When homologues pair at the wrong copy, crossing over between the mispaired repeats, non-allelic homologous recombination, deletes (or duplicates) exactly the segment between them. The repeats set the breakpoints, so the same deletion arises again and again as a new mutation.

Q4. An individual is heterozygous for a paracentric inversion. One crossover happens inside the inversion loop in a meiosis. What are the four products of that meiosis, and how many of them can give a normal offspring?

Hint

Two chromatids take part in the crossover, two do not.

Answer

The two non-crossover chromatids are one normal and one inverted chromosome, both balanced. The two crossover chromatids become a dicentric chromosome (with two centromeres, torn apart at anaphase I) and an acentric fragment (lost). Only the two non-crossover products give viable, balanced gametes, so at most 2 of 4. That is why recombinants inside the inversion are missing among the offspring.

Q5. How do the crossover products differ if the inversion is pericentric?

Hint

Where is the centromere? Count centromeres in each crossover product.

Answer

Each crossover chromatid has one centromere, so there is no dicentric or acentric. But each has a duplication of one end of the chromosome and a deficiency of the other end. Gametes with them are unbalanced, and often give inviable zygotes. The result is the same: the crossover products are lost.

Q6. A healthy man is a carrier of a balanced reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 4 and 11. Explain why his partner has had several miscarriages, and what kinds of gametes he makes.

Hint

Two of four chromosomes go to each pole. Which combinations are balanced?

Answer

His four chromosomes (normal 4, normal 11, and the two translocated ones) pair as a cross at meiosis I. Alternate segregation gives balanced gametes: either both normal chromosomes, or both translocated ones (a balanced carrier like him). Adjacent-1 and adjacent-2 segregation give gametes with a duplication of one translocated segment and a deficiency of the other. Zygotes from these are unbalanced, and most miscarry; some may be born with malformations.

Q7. A child with Down syndrome has 46 chromosomes. How is that possible, and why does it matter for the parents?

Hint

Can a third copy of 21q sit on another chromosome?

Answer

The child has translocation Down syndrome: two normal chromosomes 21 plus a Robertsonian translocation chromosome that carries a third copy of 21q, fused to another acrocentric (for example rob(14;21)). The total count is 46, but there are three copies of 21q. One parent may be a balanced carrier (45 chromosomes), so the recurrence risk in the family is much higher than for ordinary trisomy 21. Both parents should be offered a karyotype.

Q8. At markers near the centromere of chromosome 21, a child with trisomy 21 has two different alleles from the mother. In which maternal meiotic division did nondisjunction most likely occur? And if the two maternal alleles were identical?

Hint

Which division separates homologues, and which separates sister chromatids?

Answer

Different alleles mean the child got both homologues of the mother: nondisjunction in meiosis I. Identical alleles mean two copies of the same homologue, that is, two sister chromatids: nondisjunction in meiosis II. Markers near the centromere are used because crossing over can make sister chromatids differ further out on the arm.

Q9. The risk of trisomy 21 rises steeply with the mother’s age, but the risk of 45,X (Turner syndrome) does not. Suggest a reason for each.

Hint

How long does an oocyte wait in meiosis I? Which parent’s chromosome is usually missing in 45,X?

Answer

Human oocytes start meiosis I before the mother is born and stay arrested in prophase I until ovulation, up to about 50 years. The cohesin that holds homologues together is not renewed and wears out, so older oocytes are more likely to nondisjoin in meiosis I. Most trisomy 21 is of maternal origin. In 45,X, the missing sex chromosome is usually the paternal one (lost in the father’s meiosis or early after fertilisation), so the mother’s age has little effect.

Q10. Why do individuals with 47,XXY or 47,XXX have far milder phenotypes than individuals with a trisomy of an autosome of similar size?

Hint

What happens to extra X chromosomes in a cell?

Answer

X inactivation: in every cell, all X chromosomes except one are silenced and condensed into Barr bodies, so most extra X genes are not expressed. (A few genes escape inactivation, which explains the remaining symptoms.) The Y carries few genes. Autosomes have no such dosage compensation, so an extra autosome raises the dose of hundreds of genes.

Q11. Bread wheat is hexaploid (2n = 6x = 42, AABBDD) and fully fertile. A triploid banana (2n = 3x = 33) is almost sterile. Explain the difference.

Hint

How many partners does each chromosome have in meiosis?

Answer

Wheat is an allopolyploid: each chromosome pairs only with its exact partner from the same ancestral genome (A with A, B with B, D with D), so meiosis forms 21 bivalents and every gamete gets one complete ABD set. In a triploid, each chromosome has three homologues; each trivalent sends 2 to one pole and 1 to the other at random, so a gamete is balanced only if all 11 chromosome types happen to go the same way: probability 2 × (½)11 ≈ 0.001.
