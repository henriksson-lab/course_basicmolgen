Exercise: genetics as information

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • use the words gene, locus, allele, genotype, phenotype, homozygous, heterozygous, wild type, mutant, dominant and recessive correctly;
  • follow one gene, HBB, from a base change in the DNA to the sickle-cell phenotype;
  • explain why “dominant” and “recessive” depend on which phenotype you look at;
  • explain why genome size and gene number are not proportional (the C-value paradox);
  • tell orthologs from paralogs, and explain why model organisms are useful;
  • look up a gene in NCBI Gene and Ensembl and read its chromosome, location, transcripts and orthologs;
  • read a CSV file into R, add a column with mutate, and plot on log-scale axes with text labels.

There is no demo in this unit. Do the pen-and-paper part first.

Background

Recap from 5MO103. Information flows DNA → RNA → protein → phenotype. A gene is a DNA segment that encodes a functional product. Its position on a chromosome is its locus.

Vocabulary.

  • An allele is one version of a gene. A diploid individual carries two alleles at each autosomal locus.
  • Genotype: the alleles carried, e.g. AA, Aa, aa. Phenotype: what is observed or measured.
  • Homozygous: two identical alleles. Heterozygous: two different alleles.
  • Wild type (w+): the standard allele in a lab population. Mutant: a changed allele.
  • If Aa has the same phenotype as AA, then A is dominant and a is recessive for that phenotype. In this unit we assume complete dominance; Extensions to Mendel adds the other cases.

Sickle-cell disease. The human gene HBB (chromosome 11) encodes β-globin. In the sickle allele HbS, codon 6 changes from GAG to GTG, and glutamate (Glu) becomes valine (Val). Databases count the start methionine too, so they write this change as c.20A>T, p.Glu7Val.

Genomes. Genome size (in megabases, Mb = 106 bp) varies more than 10,000-fold across life, but the number of protein-coding genes varies far less. Most of a large genome is non-coding: introns, intergenic DNA, repeats such as transposable elements. Some genomes are also large because of polyploidy.

Orthologs are genes in different species that descend from one gene in their common ancestor. Paralogs arise by duplication within one genome.

A small part of the genetic code, enough for this exercise:

Codon Amino acid Codon Amino acid
ATG Met (M) CCT Pro (P)
GTG Val (V) GAG Glu (E)
CAT His (H) AAG Lys (K)
CTG Leu (L) GCG Ala (A)
ACT Thr (T)

By hand

B1. (vocabulary) For each genotype, say whether it is homozygous or heterozygous, and give the phenotype. In the fly, w+ (red eyes) is dominant to w (white eyes).

  1. w+/w+    b. w+/w    c. w/w

Which allele is the wild type, and which is the mutant?

B2. (gene vs allele) Fill in the blanks with gene, allele, locus, genotype or phenotype: “HBB is a ___ at a ___ on chromosome 11. HbA and HbS are two s of it. A person with the HbS/HbS has the ___ sickle-cell disease.”

B3. (translate) The coding sequence of HBB starts like this (coding strand, 5′→3′):

ATG GTG CAT CTG ACT CCT GAG GAG AAG
  1. Translate it with the codon table above.
  2. The start Met is removed from the mature protein. Number the remaining amino acids from 1 (so Val = 1). Which amino acid is number 6?
  3. Now number the nucleotides of the coding sequence from 1 (A of ATG = 1). Which nucleotide is number 20? Change it from A to T and translate again. Which codon and which amino acid changed? Write the change in both the traditional form (Glu6Val) and the database form (p.Glu7Val).

B4. (another allele) A different HBB allele, HbC, has the change c.19G>A. Using the sequence in B3, which codon changes, what is the new codon, and what is the new amino acid? Write the change in the database form p.Xxx#Yyy.

B5. (dominance depends on the phenotype) For HbA/HbA, HbA/HbS and HbS/HbS, the following is observed:

HbA/HbA HbA/HbS HbS/HbS
Sickle-cell disease no no yes
Haemoglobin bands on a gel HbA HbA and HbS HbS

For each row, say whether HbS is dominant, recessive or codominant. Why is this not a contradiction?

B6. (genes per Mb) Use these values from NCBI Datasets: E. coli 4.6 Mb and 4,290 protein-coding genes; baker’s yeast 12.1 Mb and 6,021 genes; human 3,099.4 Mb and 20,054 genes.

  1. Compute the number of genes per Mb for each.
  2. If the human genome were packed as densely as the E. coli genome, how many Mb would it need for its 20,054 genes?
  3. What fills the rest of the human genome?

B7. (orthologs and paralogs) An ancestral gene G duplicated into G1 and G2. Later, the human and mouse lineages split, and each kept both copies: human G1, human G2, mouse G1, mouse G2.

  1. Draw the gene tree.
  2. Which pairs are orthologs, and which are paralogs?
  3. If the duplication had instead happened after the split, and only in the human lineage, what would the mouse have, and how would the orthology change?

B8. (choosing the approach) For each question, name the approach (transmission, molecular, genomic or population) and a suitable organism from: yeast, fly, mouse, human families.

  1. Do children of two HbA/HbS carriers get the disease in about 1 in 4 cases?
  2. Which genes are needed for a cell to divide? You want to test thousands of mutants quickly.
  3. Does a mouse with no working Cftr gene get lung and gut problems like people with cystic fibrosis?
  4. Is HbS more common in regions where malaria was common?

Find it

These are your first database tasks. Take them slowly and check the organism every time: searching a gene symbol gives hits in many species.

F1. NCBI Gene: HBB. Go to NCBI Gene. Type HBB human in the search box and press Search. In the results table, click the row where the Name/Gene ID is HBB and the Organism is Homo sapiens.

On the gene page, read the Summary box at the top:

  1. What is the Official Full Name?
  2. What is the Gene ID (a number)?
  3. On which chromosome is HBB? (Look for “Chromosome” in the Genomic context section, or “Location” in the summary.)
  4. What is the cytogenetic location? (The field is called “Location” or “Map location” and looks like 11p15.4.) What do the “p” and “15.4” mean?
  5. Under “Also known as”, which familiar name for the protein do you see?

F2. Ensembl: HBB. Go to Ensembl. Choose Human in the species box, type HBB and press Go. Click the gene result HBB (Human Gene). On the gene page:

  1. What is the Ensembl gene ID (it starts with ENSG)?
  2. The Location line gives the chromosome, start and end, and strand. Write it down. Is the gene on the forward or the reverse strand?
  3. Click Show transcript table. How many transcripts are listed? This number changes between Ensembl releases, so also note the release number (bottom of the page).
  4. One transcript is flagged MANE Select (and Ensembl canonical). What is its name (HBB-2xx), and how long is its protein (aa)?

F3. CFTR: chromosome and mouse ortholog. a. In NCBI Gene, search CFTR human and open the Homo sapiens entry. On which chromosome is CFTR, and what is its cytogenetic location? What is the official full name? b. In Ensembl, open human CFTR. In the left-hand menu, under Comparative Genomics, click Orthologues. Find Mouse in the table. What is the mouse gene called? What type of orthology is it (the “Type” column, e.g. 1-to-1)? What is the “Target %id”? c. Click the mouse gene. On which mouse chromosome is it?

F4. (optional) The Alliance of Genome Resources: is there a CFTR in fly or yeast? Go to the Alliance of Genome Resources, search CFTR and open the Homo sapiens gene. Scroll to Orthology.

  1. With the default (stringent) filter, which species are listed?
  2. Change the stringency filter to the least strict setting (“No filter”/“All”). Do any fly (D. melanogaster) or yeast (S. cerevisiae) genes appear? How many prediction methods support them?
  3. What does this tell you about whether fly or yeast has a true CFTR ortholog?

In R

The data for this part is in data/genomes.csv (download it and put it in a data/ folder next to your script). It lists ten organisms with genome size and protein-coding gene count. Every value comes from the NCBI Datasets reference genome and its current annotation, and the source column gives the assembly accession for each row.

New idiom: log-scale axes and text labels. When values span many orders of magnitude (here 4.6 to 29,118 Mb), a linear axis squashes most points into a corner. scale_x_log10() and scale_y_log10() space the axis by powers of ten instead, so 10, 100 and 1,000 are equally far apart. geom_text(aes(label = ...)) writes a label at each point, so you can see which organism is which without a legend.

Hints

Hint B3: counting codons and nucleotides

Write the sequence in triplets and number the codons 1, 2, 3, … from ATG. Codon n covers nucleotides \(3n-2\) to \(3n\). So nucleotide 20 is in codon 7 (nucleotides 19–21). Codon 7 counting the Met is amino acid 6 without the Met.

Hint B7: orthologs and paralogs

Find the node in the tree where two genes last shared an ancestor. If that node is a speciation (the human–mouse split), the genes are orthologs. If it is a duplication, they are paralogs.

Hint F1–F3: finding your way in NCBI Gene and Ensembl

In NCBI Gene, the search result is a table with columns Name/Gene ID, Description, Location and Organism. The Location column already shows the cytogenetic band. In Ensembl, the gene page has a left-hand menu; Orthologues is under Comparative Genomics. If you land on a “Search results” page, make sure you click a Gene result for Human.

Hint R5c: filter and summarise

Use filter() to keep group == "animal", then summarise() with max(genome_mb) / min(genome_mb) and max(protein_coding_genes) / min(protein_coding_genes).

Solutions

Solutions: By hand

B1. a. w+/w+: homozygous, red eyes. b. w+/w: heterozygous, red eyes (w+ is dominant). c. w/w: homozygous, white eyes. w+ is the wild type; w is the mutant.

B2. “HBB is a gene at a locus on chromosome 11. HbA and HbS are two alleles of it. A person with the genotype HbS/HbS has the phenotype sickle-cell disease.”

B3. a. ATG GTG CAT CTG ACT CCT GAG GAG AAG → Met Val His Leu Thr Pro Glu Glu Lys. b. Without Met: Val(1) His(2) Leu(3) Thr(4) Pro(5) Glu(6) Glu(7) Lys(8). Amino acid 6 is glutamate. c. Nucleotides 19–21 are codon 7 (GAG), so nucleotide 20 is the A in the middle of GAG. A→T gives GTG, which codes for Val. The protein becomes Met Val His Leu Thr Pro Val Glu Lys. Traditional name: Glu6Val; database name: c.20A>T, p.Glu7Val (the same change, counted with the Met).

B4. c.19 is the first base of codon 7 (GAG). G→A gives AAG, which codes for Lys. The change is p.Glu7Lys (traditionally Glu6Lys). This is the haemoglobin C allele; ClinVar lists NM_000518.4(HBB):c.19G>A (p.Glu7Lys).

B5. Disease row: the heterozygote looks like HbA/HbA, so HbS is recessive for disease. Gel row: the heterozygote shows both bands, so the alleles are codominant for the haemoglobin protein. It is not a contradiction because dominance is a statement about one phenotype. Each allele makes its own protein (codominance at the protein level), but half normal haemoglobin is enough to avoid disease under normal conditions (recessive at the level of disease).

B6. a. E. coli: \(4{,}290 / 4.6 \approx 933\) genes per Mb. Yeast: \(6{,}021 / 12.1 \approx 498\). Human: \(20{,}054 / 3{,}099.4 \approx 6.5\). b. \(20{,}054 / 933 \approx 21.5\) Mb, which is less than 1% of the real 3,099 Mb. c. Introns, large intergenic regions, and repeated sequences (especially transposable elements), plus centromeres, telomeres, regulatory DNA and genes for non-coding RNAs.

B7. a. The tree has a duplication node at the root (G → G1, G2). Below each of G1 and G2 is a speciation node (human, mouse). b. Orthologs: human G1–mouse G1, and human G2–mouse G2 (they last shared an ancestor at the speciation). Paralogs: human G1–human G2, mouse G1–mouse G2, and also human G1–mouse G2 and human G2–mouse G1 (their last common ancestor is the duplication). c. The mouse would have a single gene G. Both human G1 and human G2 would be orthologs of mouse G: a one-to-many (in Ensembl, “1-to-many”) orthology. Human G1 and G2 would be paralogs of each other. This is roughly what has happened in the β-globin cluster, which is why there is no simple 1-to-1 mouse ortholog of human HBB.

B8. a. Transmission, human families (a pedigree or family study). b. Molecular/transmission genetics in yeast: a fast, single-celled organism where thousands of mutants can be screened. (A genomic screen, testing every gene, also fits.) c. Molecular, in the mouse: knock out the Cftr ortholog and look at the phenotype. The mouse is a mammal with lungs and a gut similar to ours. d. Population, in humans: compare HbS allele frequencies between populations.

Solutions: Find it

All facts checked in November 2026 (NCBI Gene; Ensembl release 116 on GRCh38; Alliance of Genome Resources).

F1. NCBI Gene, HBB. Click path: NCBI Gene → search HBB human → row HBB, Homo sapiens → Gene ID 3043. a. Official full name: hemoglobin subunit beta. b. Gene ID: 3043. c. Chromosome 11. d. Location 11p15.4: “p” is the short arm of chromosome 11 (“q” is the long arm), and 15.4 is the band (region 1, band 5, sub-band 4) counted outwards from the centromere. HBB lies near the tip of the short arm. e. Also known as: beta-globin (other aliases listed are CD113t-C and ECYT6). The gene has 3 exons.

F2. Ensembl, HBB. Click path: Ensembl → Human → HBB → Go → HBB (Human Gene) → ENSG00000244734. a. ENSG00000244734. b. Chromosome 11: 5,225,464–5,229,395, reverse strand (GRCh38). The gene is read from right to left on the standard chromosome map. c. In release 116 the transcript table lists 19 transcripts (most protein-coding, some “nonsense mediated decay” or “retained intron”). Your number may differ in a later release, which is why we note the release. Many extra transcripts come from new long-read evidence; most of the protein is made from one main transcript. d. HBB-201 (ENST00000335295) is the MANE Select and Ensembl canonical transcript. It matches RefSeq NM_000518.5 and encodes 147 aa (146 aa in the mature chain, after the start Met is removed).

Optional observation: if you open Orthologues for HBB, Ensembl lists orthologs in many species, but no mouse gene. The β-globin genes duplicated separately in the primate and rodent lineages, so mouse Hbb-bs and Hbb-bt do not pair one-to-one with human HBB (compare B7c). That is why F3 uses CFTR for the mouse ortholog.

F3. CFTR. a. Click path: NCBI Gene → CFTR human → Gene ID 1080. Chromosome 7, location 7q31.2 (long arm). Official full name: CF transmembrane conductance regulator (the older name “cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator” is still widely used). b. Click path: Ensembl → Human → CFTR → ENSG00000001626 → Comparative Genomics → Orthologues → Mouse. The mouse gene is Cftr (ENSMUSG00000041301). Type: 1-to-1. Target %id: about 79% (78.7% in release 116), meaning about 79% of the mouse protein’s amino acids are identical to the aligned human ones. c. Mouse Cftr is on mouse chromosome 6 (GRCm39). Chromosome numbers are not shared between species.

F4. Alliance of Genome Resources. Click path: alliancegenome.org → search CFTR → CFTR (Homo sapiens, HGNC:1884) → Orthology. a. With the stringent default filter, only vertebrates are listed: zebrafish cftr, mouse Cftr, rat Cftr, and frog (Xenopus tropicalis cftr; X. laevis cftr.L and cftr.S). b. With no filter, many fly and yeast genes appear: in fly a gene actually named Cftr, plus Mrp4, Mrp5, Sur and several CG-numbered genes; in yeast YOR1, YCF1, STE6, YBT1 and others. Each is supported by only one or two prediction methods (e.g. Ensembl Compara, PANTHER), against about ten for the mouse ortholog. c. Fly and yeast have related ABC-transporter genes, the same protein family as CFTR, but no high-confidence one-to-one ortholog. Gene families are conserved across all eukaryotes; a specific member like CFTR may only be clearly traceable among vertebrates.

R6. Example answer: Genome size varies more than 6,000-fold in this table, but gene number only about 25-fold, and among animals 290-fold against 2-fold. Large genomes are not large because they have more genes, but because they have more non-coding DNA: long introns, large intergenic regions and especially repeated sequences such as transposable elements. Polyploidy (wheat) adds whole extra genomes, and with them extra gene copies. So the amount of DNA (the C-value) says little about the number of genes or the complexity of the organism.
