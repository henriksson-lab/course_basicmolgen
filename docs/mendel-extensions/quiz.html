Quiz: extensions to Mendel

Short questions on dominance, multiple alleles, lethal alleles, penetrance and epistasis. Try each on paper first, then open the answer. For the two-gene questions, start from the 16 squares of a dihybrid Punnett square: 9 A_B_ : 3 A_bb : 3 aaB_ : 1 aabb.

Q1. Red snapdragons crossed with white snapdragons give an all-pink F₁. What phenotypes, in what ratio, do you expect in the F₂? Why does this show that the parental alleles did not blend?

Hint

With incomplete dominance, each genotype has its own phenotype. What is the genotype ratio of an F₂?

Answer

1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. The F₁ is CRCW, and the F₂ genotypes are 1 CRCR : 2 CRCW : 1 CWCW. With incomplete dominance the phenotype ratio equals the genotype ratio. Red and white come back unchanged in the F₂, so the alleles stayed separate in the pink F₁. Only the phenotype of the heterozygote was intermediate.

Q2. What is the difference between incomplete dominance and codominance? Give one example of each.

Hint

Is the heterozygote an average of the two homozygotes, or does it show both of them?

Answer

In incomplete dominance the heterozygote is intermediate (pink snapdragons, from red × white). In codominance the heterozygote shows both parental phenotypes in full (blood type AB: IAIB red cells carry both A and B antigens). In both cases the F₂ phenotype ratio is 1 : 2 : 1.

Q3. A mother has blood type A and a father has blood type B. Their first child has type O. What are the parents’ genotypes, and what is the probability that their next child has type AB?

Hint

A type O child is ii. Where did each i come from?

Answer

The child is ii, so each parent carries i: the mother is IAi and the father IBi. The next child gets IA from the mother with probability 1/2 and IB from the father with probability 1/2, so P(AB) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. (The four blood types A, B, AB and O are equally likely from this cross.)

Q4. Most loss-of-function alleles are recessive. Explain why, and name two ways in which a mutant allele can still be dominant.

Hint

How much protein does a heterozygote make, and is that usually enough?

Answer

A heterozygote for a null allele still makes about half the normal protein, and for most genes that is enough for a normal phenotype (the gene is haplosufficient). A mutant allele can be dominant through any of:

  • haploinsufficiency: half the protein is not enough;
  • dominant negative: the mutant protein spoils the normal protein, for example in a dimer or a collagen triple helix;
  • gain of function: the mutant protein is over-active or does something new, as with FGFR3 in achondroplasia.

Q5. A protein works as a dimer. A heterozygote makes normal and dominant-negative subunits in equal amounts, and they pair at random. What fraction of the dimers is fully normal? Compare with a heterozygote for a null allele that makes no protein.

Hint

Each subunit in a dimer is normal with probability 1/2. Use the product rule.

Answer

A dimer is fully normal only if both subunits are normal: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. A null heterozygote makes only normal subunits, at half the amount, so it has 1/2 of the normal amount of working dimer. The dominant-negative allele does more damage than losing the gene altogether. (For a tetramer, the fraction would be (1/2)⁴ = 1/16.)

Q6. Yellow mice crossed with yellow mice give 2 yellow : 1 agouti, never 3 : 1. Explain. What would you expect from yellow × agouti?

Hint

Which genotype class of a 1 : 2 : 1 is missing?

Answer

All yellow mice are AYa. Yellow × yellow gives 1 AYAY : 2 AYa : 1 aa, but AYAY embryos die. The survivors are 2 yellow : 1 agouti. AY is dominant for coat colour and recessive for lethality (pleiotropy). Yellow × agouti (AYa × aa) gives 1 yellow : 1 agouti, with no deaths, because no AYAY offspring can form.

Q7. A dominant allele causes a disorder with 80% penetrance. A heterozygous parent and a homozygous normal parent have a child. What is the probability that the child is affected?

Hint

Two things must both happen: the child must inherit the allele, and the allele must show.

Answer

P(inherits the allele) = 1/2. P(affected | has the allele) = 0.8. P(affected) = 1/2 × 0.8 = 0.4. So 40% of children are affected and 10% carry the allele without showing it, which is how a dominant trait can skip a generation.

Q8. What is the difference between dominance and epistasis?

Hint

How many genes are involved in each?

Answer

Dominance is a relation between two alleles of the same gene: which allele’s phenotype shows in the heterozygote. Epistasis is a relation between two different genes: the genotype at one gene hides the effect of the other gene (as ee hides B/b in Labrador coat colour).

Q9. Two pure-breeding white-flowered sweet pea lines are crossed. The F₁ is all purple. The F₁ is selfed and gives 9 purple : 7 white. Explain with a pathway, and give the genotypes of the two white parents.

Hint

Which of the 16 squares are purple? What must the F₁ have that neither parent has?

Answer

Colour needs two steps in a row, enzyme A and then enzyme B (colourless → colourless → purple). Only A_B_ plants (9/16) make pigment. A_bb, aaB_ and aabb are all white (3 + 3 + 1 = 7). The parents were AAbb and aaBB. Each lacks a different enzyme, so the F₁ AaBb has both and is purple: the two white mutations complement each other.

Q10. In summer squash, WwYy × WwYy gives white, yellow and green fruit in 12 : 3 : 1. Which genotypes are green, and which gene is epistatic?

Hint

Which class of 16ths is the “1”?

Answer

Green is the 1/16 class, wwyy. Yellow is wwY_ (3/16). Everything with at least one W is white (W_Y_ + W_yy = 9 + 3 = 12). The dominant W allele is epistatic to Y/y (dominant epistasis). Y only shows its effect in ww plants.

Q11. An F₂ from a dihybrid cross has 151 plants with triangular seed capsules and 9 with ovoid capsules. Which ratio would you test first, and why?

Hint

Convert to 16ths: count ÷ total × 16.

Answer

The total is 160. In 16ths: 151/160 × 16 = 15.1 and 9/160 × 16 = 0.9. So test 15 : 1 (duplicate genes): only aabb lacks the product. Expected counts are 150 and 10, and χ² = 1²/150 + 1²/10 = 0.107, df = 1, p ≈ 0.74, which fits. (This is the shepherd’s purse pattern.)

Q12. A dihybrid F₂ has three phenotype classes in 9 : 3 : 4. A student says: “9 : 7 also fits, with p = 0.6, so both models are fine.” What is wrong with this?

Hint

How do you get 9 : 7 from 9 : 3 : 4?

Answer

9 : 7 is 9 : 3 : 4 with the “3” and the “4” merged. If the finer ratio fits, the coarser one will usually fit too, because merging classes throws information away. But you can see three phenotypes, and 9 : 7 predicts only two. A model has to explain every phenotype class you can tell apart. So 9 : 3 : 4 is the answer, and χ² alone cannot make that choice for you.
