Exercise: Development & model organisms

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • tell forward from reverse genetics, and say what each can and cannot find;
  • explain how a balancer keeps a recessive lethal mutation in a stock, and plan the generations of a zygotic and a maternal-effect screen;
  • predict embryo phenotypes from the mother’s genotype for a maternal-effect gene such as bicoid;
  • classify segmentation mutants as gap, pair-rule or segment polarity, and reason about a stripe enhancer;
  • explain homeotic transformations and Hox colinearity, and why a Hox gain of function is dominant;
  • order genes in a developmental pathway by epistasis;
  • find a developmental gene and its orthologs in NCBI Gene, MGI, FlyBase and the Alliance of Genome Resources.

Background

Forward vs reverse genetics. Forward genetics goes from phenotype to gene: mutagenise, screen, then map and clone. Reverse genetics goes from gene to phenotype: knock out or knock down a chosen gene (RNAi, CRISPR) and look. Forward screens are unbiased but miss genes that are redundant or have no visible phenotype. Reverse genetics can show “no phenotype” when a paralog covers for the gene.

Balancers. A balancer chromosome carries several inversions, so it cannot give recombinant products with its homologue. It also carries a dominant marker, such as Curly wings, and a recessive lethal. A stock m/Balancer therefore breeds true: both kinds of homozygote die.

Maternal effect. For a maternal-effect gene, the embryo’s phenotype depends on the mother’s genotype, not its own. The mother loads mRNAs such as bicoid into the egg. Screening for maternal-effect mutants needs homozygous mothers, so it takes one generation more than a zygotic screen.

The segmentation hierarchy. Maternal gradients (Bicoid, Nanos) → gap genes (broad domains; a mutant lacks a block of adjacent segments) → pair-rule genes (7 stripes; a mutant lacks every other segment) → segment-polarity genes (14 stripes; part of each segment is replaced by a mirror image of the rest) → Hox genes (give each segment its identity). Each even-skipped stripe has its own enhancer. Stripe 2 forms where the activators Bicoid and Hunchback are high and the repressors Giant and Krüppel are low.

Hox genes. Homeotic mutations turn one body part into another. The order of Hox genes on the chromosome matches the order of the body regions where they act, from anterior to posterior (colinearity). The Drosophila order is lab, pb, Dfd, Scr, Antp, Ubx, abd-A, Abd-B. Mammals have four Hox clusters.

Epistasis in a pathway. For a pathway where gene A inhibits gene B, which promotes an outcome: when single mutants give opposite phenotypes, the double mutant shows the phenotype of the downstream gene. This is the same logic as in Extensions to Mendel.

By hand

B1. (forward or reverse?) Classify each approach as forward or reverse genetics, and name one limitation of each.

  1. Mutagenise zebrafish with ENU and look for embryos with heart defects.
  2. Inject Drosophila embryos with double-stranded RNA against a gene known to be expressed in the wing.
  3. Make a mouse knockout of the mouse ortholog of a human disease gene.
  4. Screen C. elegans for mutants in which cells that should die survive.

B2. (balancers) A recessive embryonic-lethal mutation m on chromosome 2 is kept in the stock m/CyO. CyO is a balancer with the dominant marker Curly (curled wings), and it is itself lethal as a homozygote.

  1. Cross m/CyO × m/CyO. What fraction of the zygotes have each genotype, and which survive?
  2. What do all the surviving adults look like? Why does this stock never need to be checked for loss of m?
  3. Why does it matter that CyO carries inversions?

B3. (planning a screen) You mutagenise males and want recessive mutations on chromosome 2 that affect the embryo’s segment pattern.

  1. Using a balancer, outline the generations from the mutagenised male (P) until you can see homozygous mutant embryos. In which generation do you look, and what fraction of the embryos in a line are homozygous?
  2. Now you want maternal-effect mutations instead. In which generation can you first score the phenotype? Explain why it is one generation later.

B4. (maternal effect: bicoid) bcd is a recessive maternal-effect mutation. Embryos from bcd/bcd mothers lack head and thorax and die.

  1. bcd/+ female × bcd/+ male: what fraction of the embryos are abnormal?
  2. Among the surviving adult daughters from (a), what fraction are bcd/bcd? What happens to their embryos, whatever the father’s genotype?
  3. bcd/bcd female × +/+ male, and +/+ female × bcd/bcd male: predict the embryos of each cross. What does the difference between these reciprocal crosses tell you?

B5. (classifying segmentation mutants) Larval cuticles of three new mutants:

  • mutant X: denticle belts are missing in every other segment;
  • mutant Y: thorax and the first abdominal segments are missing, and the rest is normal;
  • mutant Z: there are 14 segments, but in each one the posterior half is replaced by a mirror image of the anterior half.

Which class is each mutant? Name one known gene of each class.

B6. (a stripe enhancer) The eve stripe 2 enhancer is activated by Bicoid and Hunchback and repressed by Giant (anterior to the stripe) and Krüppel (posterior to it).

  1. In a Krüppel mutant embryo, how would stripe 2 change? (Look only at stripe 2.)
  2. A fly is heterozygous for a small deletion of the stripe 2 enhancer on one copy of eve. Is eve expressed in the stripe 2 region? From which copy? Use the words cis and trans.
  3. A reporter gene with only the stripe 2 enhancer in front of lacZ is put into flies. Where is β-galactosidase made?

B7. (Hox genes)

  1. Antennapedia gain-of-function alleles (legs instead of antennae) are dominant, while loss-of-function alleles of most Hox genes are recessive. Explain the difference.
  2. Using colinearity and the gene order in the Background, which Drosophila Hox gene acts furthest posterior? Which acts furthest anterior?
  3. Mammals have four Hox clusters. Why do single Hox knockouts in mice often give only mild changes in the vertebrae?

B8. (ordering a pathway by epistasis) In C. elegans, 131 cells die by programmed cell death during development.

  • ced-3 loss of function: no cell deaths.
  • ced-9 loss of function: many extra cells die, and the embryo dies.
  • ced-9; ced-3 double mutant: no cell deaths.
  • ced-4 loss of function: no cell deaths. The ced-9; ced-4 double mutant also has no cell deaths.

Order ced-3, ced-4 and ced-9 in a pathway leading to cell death, using arrows (→, activates) and ⊣ (inhibits).

B9. (PAX6 and dose) Aniridia (no iris) is autosomal dominant and caused by loss of one copy of PAX6 (haploinsufficiency). Homozygous loss of PAX6 gives no eyes and severe brain defects, and is usually lethal.

  1. Two people with aniridia, each PAX6/−, have children. What are the probabilities of a child with normal eyes, with aniridia, and with complete loss of PAX6?
  2. Mouse Small eye (Sey) behaves the same way. How would you expect Sey/+ × Sey/+ litters to look at birth?

B10. (from a mosaic founder to a homozygous mutant) A CRISPR knockout founder (F0) is usually a mosaic: different cells carry different edits, or none. Explain how you get homozygous mutants of one defined allele from such a founder, and in which generation they first appear. What fraction of that generation’s offspring is homozygous?

Find it

F1. (NCBI Gene and Ensembl) Search NCBI Gene for human PAX6. Give the Gene ID and the cytogenetic location. Then search Ensembl for the same gene and give its Ensembl gene ID (it starts with ENSG).

F2. (MGI, mouse) Search MGI for mouse Pax6. Which chromosome is it on? In the gene’s allele list, find the classic spontaneous allele behind the Small eye phenotype and write its allele symbol.

F3. (FlyBase) In FlyBase, look up eyeless (ey), twin of eyeless (toy), Ultrabithorax (Ubx) and labial (lab). For each, give the chromosome or chromosome arm. Give the FlyBase gene ID (FBgn…) of ey and of Ubx.

F4. (the Alliance of Genome Resources) Open the human PAX6 page at the Alliance of Genome Resources and scroll to Orthology.

  1. With the default (stringent) filter, which fly gene is listed as an ortholog? Is ey there?
  2. Relax the filter. Which fly gene appears now, and how many methods support it?
  3. List the zebrafish and worm orthologs and their chromosomes. Use ZFIN and WormBase if the Alliance page does not show the chromosome.
  4. Now open the fly gene lab and list its stringent human orthologs. What do they have in common?
Hint for F4

The orthology table has a stringency setting above it. “Best” or “stringent” keeps only calls supported by several methods and marked as best matches. Ortholog calls are predictions made by algorithms, so different methods and databases can disagree.

Solutions

Solutions: By hand

B1.

  1. Forward. It finds only genes whose loss gives a visible, specific heart phenotype; it misses redundant genes and genes needed earlier for survival.
  2. Reverse (knockdown). The knockdown may be incomplete, and the dsRNA may hit other genes with similar sequence (off-target effects).
  3. Reverse. The mouse ortholog may be redundant with a paralog, so the knockout can show no phenotype; or the knockout can be lethal, which hides later roles. A conditional (Cre/lox) knockout gets round that.
  4. Forward. This is the screen that found the ced genes. It needs a way to see the surviving cells, and it misses genes that are essential for viability.

B2.

  1. ¼ m/m (die as embryos), ½ m/CyO, ¼ CyO/CyO (die).
  2. Every surviving adult is m/CyO and has Curly wings. Because both homozygotes die, every survivor must carry m. The stock cannot lose the mutation without anyone selecting for it.
  3. Without the inversions, crossing over between CyO and the m chromosome would make recombinants that separate m from its neighbours, or that move the lethal and the marker apart. The inversions make crossover products unbalanced, so they do not survive, and the chromosome stays intact.

B3.

  1. P: mutagenised male × balancer female. F1: individual males, each carrying one mutagenised chromosome (m*) over a balancer. Cross each F1 male to the balancer stock again, to found a line. F2: collect m*/Balancer brothers and sisters in each line (identified by the marker). F2 × F2 within the line gives F3 embryos, of which ¼ are m*/m*. You score the F3 embryos (dead embryos with an abnormal cuticle).
  2. A maternal-effect phenotype shows only in the embryos of a homozygous mother. So the F3 m*/m* females must survive to adulthood (they look normal), and you score their embryos, the F4. That is one generation later.

B4.

  1. None. All the mothers are bcd/+, so all the eggs get bicoid mRNA. The embryos’ own genotype does not matter.
  2. ¼ of all daughters are bcd/bcd. They look normal, because their bcd/+ mother gave them bicoid mRNA. But all of their embryos lack head and thorax, whatever the father is.
  3. bcd/bcd female × +/+ male: all the embryos are abnormal, although they are bcd/+. +/+ female × bcd/bcd male: all normal. Reciprocal crosses differ, and the mother’s genotype decides. That is the signature of a maternal effect. Compare sex linkage (Part 2), organelle inheritance (Part 5) and imprinting (Part 6).

B5. - X: pair-rule (missing every other segment), e.g. even-skipped or fushi tarazu. - Y: gap (a block of adjacent segments missing), e.g. Krüppel. hunchback or knirps would also do. - Z: segment polarity (a mirror-image duplication within each segment), e.g. wingless or hedgehog.

B6.

  1. Krüppel sets the posterior border of stripe 2. Without it, stripe 2 widens posteriorly into the region where Krüppel would normally repress it.
  2. Yes. The enhancer is cis-acting: it controls only the copy of eve on the same chromosome. In the stripe 2 region, the intact copy is expressed, and the copy with the deleted enhancer is not. The transcription factors (Bicoid, Hunchback, Giant, Krüppel) are trans-acting and reach both copies, but only the intact copy has the binding sites.
  3. Only in stripe 2. The enhancer alone is enough to reproduce that one stripe, which is how enhancers are defined experimentally.

B7.

  1. A gain-of-function allele makes a normal Antp protein in the wrong place (the head). One such copy is enough to switch on a leg programme, so it is dominant. A loss-of-function allele leaves one working copy, and for most Hox genes half the dose is enough, so it is recessive.
  2. Abd-B acts furthest posterior; lab furthest anterior.
  3. The four clusters contain paralogs, for example Hoxa1, Hoxb1 and Hoxd1, which partly cover for each other (redundancy). Full transformations need double or triple knockouts of a paralog group.

B8. ced-9 ⊣ ced-4 → ced-3 → cell death. - ced-3 and ced-4 are needed for death, so loss of either means no deaths. ced-9 prevents death, so its loss means extra deaths. - The ced-9; ced-3 and ced-9; ced-4 doubles look like ced-3 and ced-4: no deaths. So ced-3 and ced-4 act downstream of ced-9. - These data alone don’t order ced-4 and ced-3 relative to each other. Biochemistry shows that CED-4 activates the caspase CED-3. Their human counterparts are BCL2 (ced-9), APAF1 (ced-4) and caspases (ced-3).

B9.

  1. PAX6/− × PAX6/−: ¼ +/+ (normal eyes), ½ +/− (aniridia), ¼ −/− (no eyes, usually lethal).
  2. About ¼ wild-type, ½ Sey/+ with small eyes, and ¼ Sey/Sey with no eyes and no nose, which die at birth. Among the survivors you see roughly 1 normal : 2 small-eyed, the same 2 : 1 logic as the yellow-mouse lethal allele in Extensions to Mendel.

B10. Cross the mosaic F0 to wild type. The F1 offspring that inherited an edited chromosome through the F0’s germ line are heterozygous for one defined allele. Sequence the F1 to find carriers of the allele you want, for example a frameshift. Cross two F1 heterozygotes carrying the same allele: in the F2, ¼ are homozygous mutants. This is Mendel’s monohybrid cross again.

Solutions: Find it

F1. NCBI Gene ID 5080, location 11p13. Ensembl gene ID ENSG00000007372. Click path: NCBI Gene → search “PAX6 human” → the top hit is PAX6, Homo sapiens; the location is in the summary box. In Ensembl, search PAX6 → Human gene.

F2. Mouse Pax6 is on chromosome 2. The Small eye allele is Pax6Sey. Click path: MGI → search Pax6 → gene detail page → Mutations, alleles, and phenotypes.

F3. - ey (FBgn0005558) and toy are both on chromosome 4, the small “dot” chromosome. - Ubx (FBgn0003944) and lab are both on 3R, in the Hox complexes (bithorax and Antennapedia complexes).

The location is in the General Information or Genomic Location section of each FlyBase gene report.

F4.

  1. With the stringent filter, the fly ortholog listed is toy (twin of eyeless). ey is not listed.
  2. With a relaxed filter, ey appears, supported by only one method. Ortholog calls are predictions. ey and toy come from a duplication in the insect lineage, and methods disagree on which one to pair with human PAX6. This is the “two paralogs” complication mentioned in the lecture.
  3. Zebrafish: pax6a on chr 25 and pax6b on chr 7, two copies from the teleost genome duplication. Worm: vab-3 on the X chromosome.
  4. lab → HOXA1, HOXB1 and HOXD1. They are all paralog group 1, the most anterior Hox genes, one in each of three of the four mammalian clusters. One fly gene has several human orthologs because the Hox cluster was duplicated in the vertebrate lineage.

Where this leads

The same genes keep coming back. The ced-9 counterpart BCL2 is an oncogene in follicular lymphoma, and Hox and PAX genes are rearranged in some cancers (Cancer genetics). The ortholog tables you just searched are the input for the R page, where you build the whole table across six species.
