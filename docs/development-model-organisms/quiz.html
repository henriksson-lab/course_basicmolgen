Quiz: Development & model organisms

Short questions on the genetic analysis of development. Try each on paper first, then open the answer.

Q1. What is the difference between forward and reverse genetics? Give one strength of each.

Hint

Which do you start from: a phenotype or a gene?

Answer

Forward genetics starts from a phenotype: random mutagenesis, screen, then identify the gene. Strength: unbiased; it finds genes nobody suspected. Reverse genetics starts from a known gene: disrupt it on purpose (RNAi, CRISPR) and look for a phenotype. Strength: targeted; you can test any gene, including ones whose mutants would never be noticed in a screen.

Q2. In the Nüsslein-Volhard–Wieschaus screen, why were the new mutations kept over a balancer chromosome?

Hint

The mutations were recessive lethals. How do you keep a lethal allele in a stock?

Answer

A recessive lethal can only be kept in heterozygotes. A balancer carries inversions that suppress recombination (so the mutation stays on its chromosome together with nearby markers), a dominant visible marker (so you can see who carries it), and its own recessive lethal (so balancer homozygotes die). A m/balancer stock then breeds true: m/m and balancer/balancer die, and only m/balancer adults survive, generation after generation, without selection.

Q3. A female fly is homozygous for a recessive maternal-effect mutation bcd. She looks normal. Her embryos, from a cross with a wild-type male, all lack head and thorax. Explain both observations.

Hint

Where does bicoid mRNA in the egg come from?

Answer

bicoid mRNA is made by the mother and placed at the anterior of the egg. The bcd/bcd female developed from an egg that her bcd/+ mother supplied with normal bicoid mRNA, so she is normal. She herself cannot supply any, so all her embryos lack the anterior gradient, whatever their own genotype (here all bcd/+).

Q4. Put these in the order in which they act: segment-polarity genes, maternal genes, Hox genes, gap genes, pair-rule genes. How many stripes do pair-rule and segment-polarity genes make?

Hint

The pattern gets finer at each step.

Answer

Maternal → gap → pair-rule (7 stripes) → segment polarity (14 stripes) → Hox (identity of each segment). (Hox genes are switched on by the gap and pair-rule genes, so they act in parallel with the later steps.)

Q5. A fly embryo lacks every other segment. Which class of gene is probably mutated? Which class if a block of several neighbouring segments is missing?

Hint

The class names describe the mutant phenotypes.

Answer

Every other segment missing: a pair-rule gene. A block of adjacent segments missing: a gap gene.

Q6. Deleting the even-skipped stripe 2 enhancer removes stripe 2 but leaves the other six stripes. What does this tell you about how eve is regulated? Is the deletion cis- or trans-acting?

Hint

How many enhancers does eve have?

Answer

eve has separate, modular enhancers for different stripes, each reading a different combination of transcription factors (stripe 2: activated by Bicoid and Hunchback, repressed by Giant and Krüppel). The deletion is cis-acting: it affects only the eve copy on the same chromosome.

Q7. Antennapedia gain-of-function alleles are dominant and give legs instead of antennae. Why are they dominant?

Hint

Is the protein abnormal, or is it in the wrong place?

Answer

The mutations (often rearrangements) put the normal Antp coding sequence under a regulatory region active in the head. One such allele is enough to make Antp protein in the antenna disc, where it switches the disc to a leg (thoracic) identity. A gain of expression in a new place is dominant: the normal allele cannot prevent it.

Q8. What is colinearity of Hox genes?

Hint

Compare the order of genes on the chromosome with the order of body regions.

Answer

The order of Hox genes along the chromosome matches the order of the body regions in which they act, from anterior (3′ end of the cluster) to posterior (5′ end). In vertebrates the 3′ genes are also expressed earlier than the 5′ genes (temporal colinearity).

Q9. Humans heterozygous for a PAX6 loss-of-function mutation have aniridia. What does this tell you about the dosage of PAX6? How would you describe the inheritance of aniridia?

Hint

Extensions to Mendel: haplosufficiency vs haploinsufficiency.

Answer

One working copy is not enough: PAX6 is haploinsufficient. Aniridia is therefore inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, although the mutant allele is a simple loss of function.

Q10. In C. elegans, ced-9 loss-of-function mutants lose many cells by extra cell death, but ced-9; ced-3 double mutants have no extra deaths. Which gene acts downstream?

Hint

Epistasis: which single-mutant phenotype does the double mutant show?

Answer

The double mutant looks like ced-3 (no deaths), so ced-3 is epistatic and acts downstream: CED-9 normally inhibits the death programme, and CED-3 (a caspase) carries it out. Without CED-3, losing the inhibitor makes no difference.

Q11. A CRISPR–Cas9 guide is designed against exon 2 of a mouse gene. Why do most edited alleles behave as null alleles, and why must the founder mice be crossed before you study the phenotype?

Hint

What does end joining usually leave behind? And are all cells of the founder edited the same way?

Answer

Cas9 cuts; error-prone end joining usually leaves a small insertion or deletion. Unless it is a multiple of 3 nt, it shifts the reading frame, giving a premature stop codon and no working protein. Founders are usually mosaics (different cells carry different edits, or none), so you cross them to get offspring that carry one defined allele in every cell, then intercross heterozygotes to get homozygotes (¼ of the progeny).

Q12. In the snail Lymnaea, dextral coiling (D) is dominant over sinistral (d) and acts as a maternal effect. A Dd snail that is sinistral is self-fertilised. What is the coiling of its offspring, and what are their genotypes?

Hint

Whose genotype decides the coiling of an offspring? Is the sinistral snail’s own genotype or phenotype the one that counts for its offspring?

Answer

All dextral, because their mother is Dd, and D is dominant. The snail itself is sinistral only because its mother was dd. The offspring genotypes are the usual 1 DD : 2 Dd : 1 dd; the dd quarter look dextral, but all their own offspring will be sinistral.

Q13. Contrabithorax is a dominant allele of Ultrabithorax that switches Ubx on in the second thoracic segment, where it is normally off. The wings become more like halteres. Is this a transformation towards a more anterior or a more posterior identity? Compare with Ubx loss of function.

Hint

Ubx normally gives T3 its identity. What does T2 become when it gets the T3 gene?

Answer

More posterior: T2 develops like T3, so the wing becomes haltere-like. A Hox gene switched on in the wrong place pushes the segment towards a more posterior identity, and one such allele is enough, so it is dominant. Ubx loss of function goes the other way: T3 falls back to T2 identity (haltere → wing), a more anterior identity, and it is recessive.

Q14. Mouse Brachyury (T) heterozygotes have short tails; T/T embryos die in mid-gestation. Among the live-born pups of T/+ × T/+, what fraction have short tails? Is T dominant or recessive?

Hint

Start from 1 : 2 : 1, remove the class that dies, and renormalise.

Answer

¼ +/+ : ½ T/+ : ¼ T/T (dead). Among survivors, ⅔ short-tailed and ⅓ normal. T is dominant for the tail phenotype (haploinsufficiency: one copy is not enough for a full tail) and recessive for lethality. Dominance describes a phenotype, not an allele.
