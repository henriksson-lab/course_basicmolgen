Quiz: human linkage & LOD scores

Short questions on linkage analysis in human families. Try each on paper first, then open the answer. Unless the question says otherwise, assume full penetrance, a phase-known affected parent, and that log10 2 = 0.301.

Q1. Give three reasons why geneticists mapping a human disease gene cannot simply compute RF = recombinants / total and test it with χ², as in Drosophila.

Hint

Think about who chooses the crosses, family size, and what we know about the parent’s chromosomes.

Answer

Any three of these. (1) No designed crosses: we cannot set up a testcross with a double heterozygote of known phase. (2) Families are small, so each gives only a few meioses, too few for χ² (expected counts below 5). (3) The phase of the affected parent is often unknown, so we cannot say which children are recombinant. (4) Not every meiosis is informative: the parent must be heterozygous at the marker. The LOD score solves this by computing a likelihood for each family and adding across families.

Q2. What makes a meiosis informative for linkage between a dominant disease gene and a marker?

Hint

You need to be able to say, for each child, which of the parent’s two homologues it received.

Answer

The parent must be heterozygous at both loci: Dd at the disease locus (an affected parent with a dominant disease) and heterozygous at the marker (e.g. 1/2). Then each child shows which disease allele (affected or not) and which marker allele it received from that parent. If the parent were 1/1 at the marker, every child would get a 1, and we could not tell recombinants from non-recombinants. It also helps if the other parent’s marker alleles differ from the affected parent’s, so that the child’s paternal allele can be read.

Q3. An affected father is D 1 / d 2 (phase known). His wife is unaffected and 3/3 at the marker. Classify each child as recombinant (R) or non-recombinant (NR): (a) affected, 1/3; (b) unaffected, 1/3; (c) affected, 2/3; (d) unaffected, 2/3.

Hint

The 3 always comes from the mother. Which combination of D/d and 1/2 did the child get from the father?

Answer
  1. D with 1: NR. (b) d with 1: R. (c) D with 2: R. (d) d with 2: NR.

Q4. Write the likelihood L(θ) for a phase-known family with 8 informative meioses and 2 recombinants. What is θ̂? What is L(0.5)?

Hint

Each meiosis is R with probability θ and NR with probability 1 − θ, independently.

Answer

L(θ) = θ2 (1 − θ)6. The maximum is at θ̂ = k/n = 2/8 = 0.25. L(0.5) = 0.58 = 1/256 ≈ 0.0039.

Q5. For a phase-known family with no recombinants, LOD(0) = n × 0.301. How many such meioses do you need to reach LOD 3? What happens to LOD(0) if a single recombinant turns up?

Hint

L(0) = 1 if k = 0. If k ≥ 1, what is 0k?

Answer

With k = 0, LOD(0) = log10(1 / 0.5n) = n log10 2 = 0.301 n. For LOD ≥ 3 you need n ≥ 3 / 0.301 = 9.97, so 10 meioses. With one recombinant, L(0) = 0, so LOD(0) = −∞: a single recombinant rules out complete linkage (θ = 0). The maximum then moves to θ̂ = 1/n.

Q6. A family gives LOD(0.1) = 1.2. In words, what does this number mean? Is it evidence of linkage?

Hint

Undo the logarithm.

Answer

101.2 ≈ 16: the family is about 16 times more probable if the disease gene and the marker are linked at θ = 0.1 than if they are unlinked. It points towards linkage, but it is far below the threshold of 3 (odds 1000 : 1). On its own it is inconclusive; add more families.

Q7. Why is the threshold for linkage as high as LOD 3, when odds of 20 : 1 would correspond to the usual 5 % level?

Hint

Before you look at any family, how likely is it that a randomly chosen marker is linked to the disease gene?

Answer

Because linkage is unlikely a priori: only about 1 marker in 50 lies close enough to a given gene to show linkage. Prior odds of about 1 : 49, multiplied by a likelihood ratio of 1000 : 1 (LOD 3), give posterior odds of roughly 20 : 1 in favour of linkage, so about a 5 % chance that the “linkage” is false. A lower threshold would give many false linkages.

Q8. Three families have maximum LODs of 1.4 (at θ = 0.05), 1.1 (at θ = 0.10) and 0.9 (at θ = 0.15). A student adds the maxima, gets 3.4, and declares linkage. What is wrong?

Hint

Which θ does each number belong to?

Answer

LODs add at the same θ. The three maxima are at different θ, so their sum is not a LOD at any single θ. Add the three curves θ by θ, then find the maximum of the sum. That maximum is always ≤ 3.4, and may well be below 3.

Q9. What does a total LOD of −2.5 at θ = 0.10 (and lower at smaller θ) tell you?

Hint

What is the threshold for exclusion?

Answer

Since LOD < −2 for every θ ≤ 0.10, linkage at θ ≤ 0.10 is excluded: the disease gene is not within about 10 cM of this marker. It does not exclude looser linkage (larger θ). A real example: four DRPLA families gave LOD −2.215 at θ = 0.15 with the HD-linked marker D4S10, showing that DRPLA is not caused by the HD gene.

Q10. Why does an unknown phase make a family less informative? Write L(θ) for a phase-unknown family with n = 6 and 1 or 5 recombinants depending on the phase.

Hint

Each phase has prior probability ½.

Answer

L(θ) = ½ θ1 (1 − θ)5 + ½ θ5 (1 − θ)1. We must average over the two possible arrangements, and the wrong one also gets weight. The maximum LOD drops from 0.63 (phase known) to 0.33. Typing the grandparents to determine phase is therefore worth a lot.

Q11. Why did microsatellites replace RFLPs as markers for human linkage mapping?

Hint

How many alleles does each have? What fraction of parents will be heterozygous?

Answer

An RFLP usually has only two alleles (site present or absent), so many parents are homozygous and their meioses are uninformative. A microsatellite (e.g. (CA)n) has many alleles of different lengths, so most people are heterozygous and almost every meiosis is informative. Microsatellites are also typed quickly by PCR instead of Southern blots.

Q12. In 1983 Gusella and colleagues showed that Huntington disease is linked to a DNA marker on chromosome 4. Why did it still take until 1993 to find the gene?

Hint

How precisely can linkage in families locate a gene? How big is 1 cM in base pairs in humans?

Answer

Linkage only gives a region: with the meioses available, a gene is placed within a few cM, which in humans is millions of base pairs holding many genes. In 1989 HD was still placed only “about 4 cM distal to D4S10”, near the tip of 4p (4p16.3). Finding the gene required more markers, a physical map of the region, and searching it for genes. In 1993 the gene (IT15, now HTT) was found to carry an expanded CAG repeat on HD chromosomes in all 75 families examined.

Q13. At θ = 0.2, how much does each non-recombinant meiosis add to the LOD, and each recombinant one? Use these terms to compute LOD(0.2) for a phase-known family with 9 non-recombinant and 1 recombinant child. (log10 1.6 = 0.204, log10 0.4 = −0.398.)

Hint

LOD(θ) = log10[L(θ) / L(0.5)] is a sum with one term per meiosis: log10[(1 − θ)/0.5] for NR, log10(θ/0.5) for R.

Answer

NR: log10(0.8 / 0.5) = log10 1.6 = +0.204. R: log10(0.2 / 0.5) = log10 0.4 = −0.398. Family: 9 × 0.204 − 0.398 = 1.836 − 0.398 = 1.44. (Check with the formula: log10 0.2 + 9 log10 0.8 + 10 × 0.301 = −0.699 − 0.872 + 3.010 = 1.44.) One recombinant cancels about two non-recombinants at this θ.
