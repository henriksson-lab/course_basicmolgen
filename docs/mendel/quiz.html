Quiz: Mendel, segregation & assortment

Short questions on monohybrid and dihybrid crosses and the two laws. Try each on paper first, then open the answer. Unless the question says otherwise, assume complete dominance and unlinked genes.

Q1. What is a true-breeding line, and why did Mendel need true-breeding parents?

Hint

What happens when a true-breeding plant is self-fertilised? What genotype must it have?

Answer

A true-breeding line gives only offspring like itself when self-fertilised, generation after generation. That means it is homozygous (RR or rr). Mendel needed this so that he knew exactly what each parent passes on: every gamete of a true-breeding round plant carries R. Without that, the F1 and F2 ratios could not be interpreted.

Q2. Mendel crossed round ♀ × wrinkled ♂ and also wrinkled ♀ × round ♂. What are these called, and what did the identical results tell him?

Hint

Which parent supplies the egg, and which the pollen?

Answer

They are reciprocal crosses. Getting the same F1 (all round) either way showed that the egg and the pollen contribute equally to the trait: it does not matter which sex the allele comes from. (This is not always true: sex-linked and mitochondrial genes, later in the course, give different results in reciprocal crosses.)

Q3. In Mendel’s F2 for seed shape there were 5474 round and 1850 wrinkled seeds. What are the expected numbers under a 3 : 1 ratio? Does the deviation prove that the model is wrong?

Hint

Total = 5474 + 1850. Multiply by ¾ and ¼.

Answer

Total = 7324. Expected: 7324 × ¾ = 5493 round and 7324 × ¼ = 1831 wrinkled. Observed is 19 fewer round and 19 more wrinkled. A small deviation like this is expected from random sampling: each seed is an independent draw, and a sample of draws almost never matches the probabilities exactly. Whether 19 is “too big” needs a test, the χ² goodness-of-fit test, in the next unit.

Q4. An F2 plant from Rr × Rr has round seeds. What is the probability that it is heterozygous?

Hint

You already know it is not rr. Of the ¾ that are round, how much is Rr?

Answer

Among all F2 plants: ¼ RR, ½ Rr, ¼ rr. Given round, only RR and Rr remain, in a ratio of ¼ : ½ = 1 : 2. So P(Rr | round) = ½ ÷ ¾ = ⅔. Mendel confirmed this: when he selfed 565 round F2 plants, 193 bred true and 372 gave both round and wrinkled offspring, close to 1 : 2.

Q5. State the law of segregation and name the event in meiosis that makes it true.

Hint

Where are the two alleles of one gene in a diploid cell?

Answer

The two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete gets one allele, and each allele goes to half of the gametes. The two alleles sit on the two homologous chromosomes, and in anaphase of meiosis I the homologues move to opposite poles. Every gamete therefore gets one homologue, and so one allele, of each pair.

Q6. A purple-flowered pea plant (purple P is dominant over white p) is crossed to a white-flowered plant. The cross gives 12 purple and 0 white offspring. What is the purple plant’s genotype? How sure can you be?

Hint

This is a testcross. If the purple plant were Pp, what is the chance that all 12 offspring are purple?

Answer

This is a testcross. The result fits PP (all offspring Pp, purple). It does not prove it: if the plant were Pp, each offspring would be purple with probability ½, so all 12 purple has probability (½)12 = 1/4096 ≈ 0.00024. That is unlikely, so PP is very probably right. One white offspring would have proved Pp.

Q7. Why is the F2 of a dihybrid cross 9 : 3 : 3 : 1? Answer in one line, using the monohybrid ratio.

Hint

Multiply (¾ + ¼) by (¾ + ¼).

Answer

Each gene on its own gives 3 : 1, and the two genes are independent, so the classes are ¾ · ¾ = 9/16, ¾ · ¼ = 3/16, ¼ · ¾ = 3/16 and ¼ · ¼ = 1/16. In short, 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 = (3 : 1) × (3 : 1). Mendel’s 315 : 108 : 101 : 32 collapses to 423 : 133 for shape and 416 : 140 for colour, both close to 3 : 1.

Q8. An Rr Yy plant makes four kinds of gamete, ¼ each. Explain this with what the chromosomes do in meiosis. When would it not be true?

Hint

Think of two homologue pairs at metaphase I. How many ways can they line up?

Answer

If R/r and Y/y are on different chromosomes, the two homologue pairs line up at metaphase I independently of each other. Half of the meioses put R and Y on the same side (giving RY and ry gametes); the other half put R with y (giving Ry and rY). Overall each of the four types is ¼. This fails when the genes are linked, that is, close together on the same chromosome. Then the parental combinations (RY, ry) are more common than ¼ each. That is the Linkage unit in Part 2.

Q9. In Aa Bb Cc × Aa Bb Cc, what fraction of the offspring is (a) aa bb cc, (b) dominant for all three traits, (c) Aa Bb Cc?

Hint

Answer each gene as its own monohybrid cross, then use the product rule.

Answer
  1. ¼ · ¼ · ¼ = 1/64. (b) ¾ · ¾ · ¾ = 27/64. (c) ½ · ½ · ½ = 1/8. A Punnett square would need 8 × 8 = 64 cells; the branch-line method or product rule needs three fractions.

Q10. A plant is heterozygous for 4 unlinked genes (Aa Bb Cc Dd). How many kinds of gamete does it make? If it is selfed, how many different genotypes can the offspring have?

Hint

Per gene: how many alleles can a gamete carry? How many genotypes can an offspring have?

Answer

Gametes: 2 choices per gene, so 24 = 16 kinds. Offspring genotypes: 3 per gene (AA, Aa, aa), so 34 = 81. With complete dominance there are 24 = 16 phenotypes, and the fully recessive class is (¼)4 = 1/256 of the offspring.

Q11. Two parents without cystic fibrosis (an autosomal recessive disease) have a child with cystic fibrosis. What are the parents’ genotypes, and what is the risk for their next child?

Hint

The child must have received a faulty allele from each parent. Which of Mendel’s crosses is this?

Answer

The child is aa, so each parent gave an a. Both parents are unaffected, so both are carriers: Aa × Aa. This is Mendel’s monohybrid F1 × F1 cross, and the risk for each child is ¼. The previous child does not change this: each child comes from a new, independent fertilisation.

Q12. “Dominant alleles are more common in the population than recessive ones.” True or false? Give an example from Mendel’s seven traits or from human disease.

Hint

What does “dominant” describe: a relation between two alleles in one individual, or a frequency in a population?

Answer

False. Dominance describes what the heterozygote looks like; it says nothing about how common an allele is. The Huntington disease allele is dominant but rare, while recessive alleles can be very common (in peas, all plants of a wrinkled line carry only r). Allele frequencies in populations are the topic of the Hardy–Weinberg unit.

Q13. List the gametes of a plant with genotype RR Yy Tt, with their proportions. How many kinds are there?

Hint

Take one gene at a time. How many different alleles can each gene contribute?

Answer

RR always gives R; Yy gives Y or y; Tt gives T or t. The gametes are RYT, RYt, RyT, Ryt, ¼ each: 4 kinds, that is 22 for the two heterozygous genes. The homozygous gene does not add any gamete types. Every gamete carries exactly one allele of each of the three genes.
