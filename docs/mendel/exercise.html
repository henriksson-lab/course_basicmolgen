Exercise: Mendel, segregation & assortment

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • predict the genotype and phenotype ratios of a monohybrid cross (1 : 2 : 1 and 3 : 1) and a testcross (1 : 1) with a Punnett square;
  • predict the outcome of a dihybrid cross (9 : 3 : 3 : 1) and a dihybrid testcross (1 : 1 : 1 : 1);
  • explain the law of segregation and the law of independent assortment in terms of what chromosomes do in meiosis;
  • use the branch-line method and the product rule for crosses with three or more genes, and count \(2^n\) gamete types and \(3^n\) genotypes;
  • look up the mode of inheritance and the gene of a human disease in OMIM (or NCBI MedGen);
  • write a small R function that uses sample() to simulate a cross, and see how observed ratios approach the expected ones as the number of offspring grows.

Work on paper first. The demo and R then show the same crosses with random sampling added.

Background

Notation. Each gene has two alleles; upper case is dominant (R, round), lower case is recessive (r, wrinkled). A diploid genotype has two alleles: RR and rr are homozygous, Rr is heterozygous. R_ means “RR or Rr”, that is, the dominant phenotype. Throughout, we assume complete dominance (the Extensions to Mendel unit relaxes this) and unlinked genes (Linkage, Part 2, relaxes this).

Law of segregation. The two alleles of a gene separate into different gametes, so an Rr plant makes ½ R and ½ r gametes. The reason is meiosis I: the two homologous chromosomes, one carrying R and one carrying r, go to opposite poles.

Law of independent assortment. Alleles of different genes go into gametes independently. An Rr Yy plant makes RY, Ry, rY, ry, ¼ each. The reason: at metaphase I, each homologue pair lines up independently of the others. This holds for genes on different chromosomes.

Key ratios.

Cross Genotypes Phenotypes
Aa × Aa 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa 3 : 1
Aa × aa (testcross) 1 Aa : 1 aa 1 : 1
AaBb × AaBb 9 genotypes, 1 : 2 : 1 : 2 : 4 : 2 : 1 : 2 : 1 9 : 3 : 3 : 1
AaBb × aabb (testcross) 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 1 : 1 : 1 : 1

Product rule. For independent genes, the probability of a combination is the product of the per-gene probabilities: P(aa bb cc) = ¼ · ¼ · ¼ = 1/64. An individual heterozygous for n genes makes \(2^n\) gamete types; a cross between two such individuals gives \(3^n\) genotypes.

Mendel’s numbers. Seed shape F2: 5474 round : 1850 wrinkled. Dihybrid F2 (shape × colour): 315 round yellow : 108 round green : 101 wrinkled yellow : 32 wrinkled green.

By hand

B1. (monohybrid, peas) In peas, yellow seeds (Y) are dominant to green (y). Mendel crossed a true-breeding yellow line with a true-breeding green line, then selfed the F1.

  1. Give the genotypes of the P plants, the F1 and the F1 gametes.
  2. Draw the Punnett square for the F2 and give the genotype and phenotype ratios.
  3. Mendel counted 8023 F2 seeds. How many yellow and green seeds do you expect? He observed 6022 yellow and 2001 green.

B2. (hidden genotypes) Among the yellow F2 seeds of B1, what fraction are YY and what fraction are Yy? Describe an experiment Mendel could do (and did) to tell them apart without a testcross.

B3. (testcross, mouse) In mice, black coat (B) is dominant to brown (b). A black male is crossed to a brown female.

  1. What offspring do you expect if the male is BB? If he is Bb?
  2. The litter has 4 black and 3 brown pups. What is the male’s genotype?
  3. Another black male sires 6 pups, all black, with brown females. Can you be sure he is BB? What is the probability of this result if he is actually Bb?

B4. (dihybrid, peas) Mendel crossed true-breeding round yellow (RR YY) with wrinkled green (rr yy) peas and selfed the F1.

  1. What gametes does the F1 make, and in what proportions? Which law are you using?
  2. Give the four F2 phenotype classes with their expected fractions.
  3. Mendel counted 556 F2 seeds. Compute the expected number in each class and compare with 315 : 108 : 101 : 32.
  4. Add up the counts for seed shape alone, and for seed colour alone. What ratios do you get?

B5. (dihybrid testcross) The F1 from B4 (Rr Yy) is crossed to a wrinkled green plant (rr yy).

  1. Give the expected offspring phenotypes and their ratio.
  2. Why does the testcross show the F1’s gametes directly?
  3. Suppose instead that R and Y were so close together on one chromosome that they never separated. What would the testcross give?

B6. (branch-line) Use the branch-line method for Rr Yy × Rr yy. Give every phenotype class and its probability.

B7. (product rule, three genes) Tall stem (T) is dominant to dwarf (t). Consider Tt Rr Yy × Tt Rr Yy.

  1. How many kinds of gamete does each parent make? How many different genotypes can the offspring have?
  2. What fraction of the offspring is tall, round and green?
  3. What fraction is tt rr yy? What fraction is Tt Rr Yy?
  4. What fraction is dominant for all three traits?

B8. (working backwards) In peas, purple flowers (P) are dominant to white (p), and axial flowers (A) are dominant to terminal (a). Two plants are crossed, giving offspring in the ratio 3 purple axial : 3 purple terminal : 1 white axial : 1 white terminal. What are the parents’ genotypes?

B9. (humans) Cystic fibrosis is autosomal recessive; Huntington disease is autosomal dominant.

  1. Two unaffected parents have a child with cystic fibrosis. What are the parents’ genotypes? What is the chance that their next child is affected? That their next two children are both unaffected?
  2. An unaffected sister of the affected child wants to know if she is a carrier. What is the probability?
  3. A man with Huntington disease (heterozygous) and an unaffected woman have a child. What is the risk for the child? Which kind of cross is this?

Solutions: Explore

E1. Each parent makes A and a, ½ each; the square has 4 cells: genotypes 1 : 2 : 1 (¼ AA, ½ Aa, ¼ aa) and phenotypes 3 : 1 (¾ A_, ¼ aa), as in B1. With N = 16, seed 1 gives exactly 12 A_ : 4 aa, but the next draws give 15 : 1 (seed 2) and 13 : 3 (seed 3). Hitting the expected count exactly is the exception, not the rule. A 3 : 1 mechanism does not guarantee a 3 : 1 sample.

E2. At N = 1000 (seed 1) there are 757 A_ : 243 aa, so the proportion of aa is 0.243. At N = 16 the proportion jumped between 1/16 = 0.06 and 4/16 = 0.25 in the first three draws. Larger samples come closer to ¼ in proportion, even though the difference in counts (here 7) can be larger than at N = 16. Among Mendel’s traits, pod colour (580 plants: 2.82 : 1) and stem length (1064 plants: 2.84 : 1) have the smallest samples and deviate most; seed shape and seed colour (7324 and 8023 seeds) are closest to 3 : 1.

E3. Each parent makes 4 gametes (AB, Ab, aB, ab, ¼ each), so the square has 4 × 4 = 16 cells. There are 9 genotypes (32), with ratio 1 : 2 : 1 : 2 : 4 : 2 : 1 : 2 : 1, and 4 phenotypes (9 : 3 : 3 : 1). The most common genotype is AaBb (4/16 = ¼): it is heterozygous for both genes, and heterozygous is the most likely outcome for each gene (½ · ½ = ¼).

E4. The aabb parent makes only one kind of gamete (ab), so there is one row. The four cells (AaBb, Aabb, aaBb, aabb) correspond one to one to the four gametes of the AaBb parent (AB, Ab, aB, ab): the testcross reads off the gametes. Expected 1 : 1 : 1 : 1, that is 25 each at N = 100, but seed 1 gives 24 : 18 : 34 : 24. Even at N = 100, a class can be off by 9 by chance.

E5. AA Bb makes AB and Ab gametes (½ each); aa bb makes only ab. Offspring: ½ AaBb (A_B_) and ½ Aabb (A_bb), so the phenotype ratio is 1 : 1, and all offspring show the dominant A phenotype. The square is 2 × 1.

Find it

The Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man database (OMIM) has one entry per human genetic disease, each with a stable six-digit MIM number. At the top of a disease entry, the Phenotype–Gene Relationships table gives the chromosome location, the Inheritance (for example AR = autosomal recessive, AD = autosomal dominant) and the Gene/Locus. OMIM sometimes asks for a login or blocks heavy use; if so, use the NCBI pages linked in each task, which give the same facts.

F1. Open the OMIM entry for cystic fibrosis, MIM 219700. In the Phenotype–Gene Relationships table, read the Inheritance column and the Gene/Locus column. What is the mode of inheritance, and which gene is it? (Alternative: NCBI MedGen, cystic fibrosis, section “Modes of inheritance”.)

F2. Do the same for Huntington disease, MIM 143100. (Alternative: NCBI MedGen, Huntington disease.)

F3. Open the NCBI Gene pages for the two genes, CFTR and HTT. In the Summary box at the top, read the Location field. On which chromosomes are the two genes? Would you expect them to assort independently in a family that carries both disease alleles?

F4. In two or three sentences each:

  1. Which of Mendel’s laws does the transmission of each disease illustrate, and which of Mendel’s crosses does a typical affected family resemble?
  2. Why can a recessive disease such as cystic fibrosis appear in a child whose parents are both unaffected, while Huntington disease (with full penetrance) almost always has an affected parent?

In R

In R we simulate crosses. The new idiom is writing your own function and using sample() to draw at random. A function packs a few lines of code under one name, so you can run them again with different inputs:

my_function <- function(input1, input2) {
  # ... code that uses input1 and input2 ...
  result            # the last value is what the function returns
}

sample(x, n, replace = TRUE) draws n values from the vector x at random, with each value equally likely and the same value allowed more than once. That is exactly what a parent does when it passes one of its two alleles to each offspring.

We use set.seed() before each simulation, so you get the same random numbers as in the solutions.

library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)

R1. (two functions) Write gametes(genotype), which splits a genotype like "Aa" into its two alleles, and cross(p1, p2, n), which simulates n offspring of a single-gene cross: each offspring gets one allele drawn at random from each parent.

gametes <- function(genotype) {
  strsplit(genotype, "")[[1]]          # "Aa" -> c("A", "a")
}

cross <- function(p1, p2, n) {
  a1 <- sample(gametes(p1), n, replace = TRUE)   # one allele from parent 1
  a2 <- sample(gametes(p2), n, replace = TRUE)   # one allele from parent 2
  # write the dominant (upper-case) allele first, so "aA" becomes "Aa"
  ifelse(a1 == toupper(a1), paste0(a1, a2), paste0(a2, a1))
}

gametes("Aa")
set.seed(1)
cross("Aa", "Aa", 16)

Count the genotypes in your 16 offspring by eye. Do you get exactly 4 : 8 : 4?

R2. (monohybrid: 1 : 2 : 1 and 3 : 1) Simulate 1000 offspring of Aa × Aa. Use table() and prop.table() to get the genotype counts and proportions, and compare them with 1 : 2 : 1. Then turn genotypes into phenotypes and compare with 3 : 1. Finally, compute the expected numbers for Mendel’s 7324 seeds and his observed ratio.

set.seed(2)
offspring <- cross("Aa", "Aa", 1000)
geno <- factor(offspring, levels = c("AA", "Aa", "aa"))   # fixes the order
table(geno)
prop.table(table(geno))

pheno <- ifelse(offspring == "aa", "aa", "A_")
prop.table(table(pheno))

7324 * c(3/4, 1/4)     # Mendel's 7324 seeds, expected round : wrinkled
5474 / 1850

R3. (testcross) Reuse cross() for the testcross Aa × aa with 1000 offspring. What ratio do you expect, and what do you get?

set.seed(3)

R4. (two genes: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1) Independent assortment means that we can simulate each gene separately and put the results side by side. Simulate 1000 offspring of AaBb × AaBb, build the phenotype of each offspring, count the four classes with count(), and compare the proportions with 9/16, 3/16, 3/16, 1/16. Then compute the expected numbers for Mendel’s 556 seeds.

set.seed(44)
n <- 1000
dihybrid <- tibble(A = cross("Aa", "Aa", n),
                   B = cross("Bb", "Bb", n)) |>
  mutate(pheno = paste0(ifelse(A == "aa", "aa", "A_"),
                        ifelse(B == "bb", "bb", "B_")),
         pheno = factor(pheno, levels = c("A_B_", "A_bb", "aaB_", "aabb")))

dihybrid |>
  count(pheno) |>
  mutate(observed = n / sum(n),
         expected = c(9, 3, 3, 1) / 16)

556 * c(9, 3, 3, 1) / 16     # Mendel's 556 seeds

R5. (more offspring, less noise) Write a function prop_aa(n) that simulates one Aa × Aa cross with n offspring and returns the proportion of aa. Run it 20 times for each of n = 16, 160, 1600 and 16000, summarise, and plot the results with ggplot2. sapply() calls a function once for each value in a vector and collects the answers; we meet it again in Part 2.

prop_aa <- function(n) {
  mean(cross("Aa", "Aa", n) == "aa")     # TRUE counts as 1, FALSE as 0
}
set.seed(5)
sims <- tibble(n = rep(c(16, 160, 1600, 16000), each = 20))
sims$prop_aa <- sapply(sims$n, prop_aa)

sims |>
  group_by(n) |>
  summarise(lowest = min(prop_aa), mean = mean(prop_aa), highest = max(prop_aa))

ggplot(sims, aes(x = n, y = prop_aa)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 1/4, linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.05, height = 0, alpha = 0.6) +
  scale_x_log10(breaks = c(16, 160, 1600, 16000)) +
  labs(x = "number of offspring (log scale)",
       y = "proportion aa",
       title = "20 simulated Aa x Aa crosses for each n") +
  theme_minimal()

How much does the spread shrink each time n grows ten-fold? Where would Mendel’s 7324 seeds sit on this plot?

R6. (three genes: the product rule) Simulate 64000 offspring of AaBbCc × AaBbCc. Check the fraction that is aa bb cc against 1/64, the fraction dominant for all three traits against 27/64, and count the distinct genotypes (compare with \(3^3\)).

set.seed(6)
n <- 64000
tri <- tibble(A = cross("Aa", "Aa", n),
              B = cross("Bb", "Bb", n),
              C = cross("Cc", "Cc", n))
mean(tri$A == "aa" & tri$B == "bb" & tri$C == "cc")
1/64
mean(tri$A != "aa" & tri$B != "bb" & tri$C != "cc")
27/64
tri |> distinct(A, B, C) |> nrow()

In every task, observed and expected are close but not equal. To decide whether a difference is small enough to be chance, we need a test: the χ² goodness-of-fit test (chisq.test()), in the next unit.

Solutions: Find it

F1. Cystic fibrosis (MIM 219700): autosomal recessive (Inheritance column: AR); gene CFTR (CF transmembrane conductance regulator, gene MIM 602421), location 7q31.2. Click path: omim.org/entry/219700 → Phenotype–Gene Relationships table at the top → Inheritance and Gene/Locus columns. On MedGen (C0010674), “Modes of inheritance” says “Autosomal recessive inheritance” and the genes section lists CFTR. (MedGen also lists modifier genes such as TGFB1; CFTR is the disease gene.)

F2. Huntington disease (MIM 143100): autosomal dominant (AD); gene HTT (huntingtin, gene MIM 613004), location 4p16.3. Click path: omim.org/entry/143100 → Phenotype–Gene Relationships table. MedGen (C0020179): “Autosomal dominant inheritance”, gene HTT.

F3. NCBI Gene → Summary → Location: CFTR is at 7q31.2 (chromosome 7) and HTT at 4p16.3 (chromosome 4). They are on different chromosomes, so they assort independently: in a parent who carries a disease allele of each, the four gamete combinations are equally likely, ¼ each.

F4. a) Both diseases show the law of segregation: an affected or carrier parent (Aa) passes the disease allele to half of the gametes. A cystic fibrosis family with two carrier parents is Mendel’s monohybrid F1 × F1 (Aa × Aa: ¼ affected). A Huntington family with one affected parent is a testcross (Aa × aa: ½ affected). b) For a recessive disease, carriers (Aa) are unaffected, because one working allele is enough. Two carrier parents each pass a with probability ½, so ¼ of their children are aa and affected, although neither parent is. For a dominant disease, one copy of the allele is enough to cause it, so a child who has the allele usually got it from an affected parent (exceptions: new mutations, or a parent who died before the late onset).

Where this leads

Every simulated cross above was a little off from its expected ratio, and so were all of Mendel’s. In Probability & the χ² test we turn “a little off” into a number: the χ² goodness-of-fit test (chisq.test() in R) asks whether a deviation like 5474 : 1850 against 3 : 1 is the size that chance usually produces. Later, Linkage (Part 2) shows what happens when two genes sit close together on one chromosome and independent assortment fails.
